State AGs Draft Joint Rules for Deepfake Political Ads

State AGs are quietly drafting joint rules to curb AI deepfake political ads ahead of 2026, a first-of-its-kind coordinated enforcement push.

Why this matters now

As the 2026 midterms approach, state attorneys general are moving beyond individual enforcement to craft joint rules targeting AI-generated deepfakes in political advertising. This unprecedented coordination—spanning multiple states—aims to harmonize disclosure standards, time-based bans, and enforcement mechanisms, filling a vacuum left by federal inaction. The Coordinated State AG Push

Multiple state attorneys general are jointly drafting rules to govern deepfake political ads, a move that marks a notable shift from isolated state statutes to a unified front ahead of the 2026 elections. The effort reportedly focuses on harmonizing disclosure requirements, time-based restrictions near elections, and enforcement standards, so that campaigns and advertisers face consistent obligations across state lines. Sources indicate that while most existing laws are enforced by Secretaries of State or AGs individually, this joint drafting could streamline cross-state enforcement and reduce loopholes where ads escape scrutiny by moving between jurisdictions.

State Law Landscape: The Patchwork Baseline

The coordinated AG effort builds on a rapidly expanding state-level patchwork. According to the StackCyber tracker, 33 states now regulate deepfakes in political campaigns, up from 28 a year earlier. Most laws require clear disclaimers on AI-altered ads or restrict deceptive deepfakes close to an election, with exceptions for satire and news. Enforcement is typically handled by the state’s Secretary of State or Attorney General, and penalties can be criminal in the worst cases. However, several laws face constitutional challenges on free speech grounds, creating uncertainty that the joint rules could partially address.

  • 33 states regulate political deepfakes, up from 28 a year earlier.
  • Common approaches: disclosure mandates and time-based bans (60–90 days before elections).
  • Enforcement typically by Secretary of State or AG, with criminal penalties possible.
  • Constitutional free-speech challenges loom over several existing statutes.
  • Federal law remains a floor, not a ceiling—no single national deepfake statute exists.

Enforcement Mechanisms and Legal Hurdles

State AGs already possess enforcement powers under existing statutes—some laws allow the AG to offer a cure period before penalties, while others permit candidates depicted by deepfakes to sue sponsors for takedowns. The coordinated rules could standardize these remedies and clarify how AI developers, payment processors, and hosting services are treated. Yet legal hurdles remain: free-speech challenges have already stalled some state laws, and courts may scrutinize joint rules as overreaching if they impose burdens on satire or legitimate news. The FCC’s authority, meanwhile, is limited to telecommunications and does not cover AI-generated videos, underscoring why state AGs are taking the lead.

  • Existing statutes give AGs varied tools—cure periods, civil suits, and criminal penalties.
  • Joint rules could harmonize remedies across states, reducing compliance friction.
  • Free-speech challenges threaten enforceability; satire/news exceptions are contentious.
  • FCC authority limited to telecom, leaving video deepfakes to state enforcement.

What the Joint Rules Could Change

If the joint rules materialize, they could introduce uniform disclosure standards, time-based windows, and a shared enforcement playbook that makes it harder for bad actors to evade scrutiny by crossing state lines. The rules might also impose obligations on platforms and ad vendors, akin to federal proposals like the Take It Down Act—which already requires online platforms to remove certain AI-generated content—but tailored to political ads. Observers note that the 2026 cycle is the first major test of these coordinated measures, with regulators scrambling as deepfakes flood the midterms.

  • Uniform standards could close interstate enforcement gaps.
  • Potential to extend obligations to platforms, payment processors, and hosting services.
  • 2026 midterms serve as the first major stress test for coordinated rules.

Closing read

Conclusion

The coordinated effort by state attorneys general to draft joint rules for deepfake political ads represents a significant escalation in state-level regulation ahead of the 2026 elections. The evidence shows a clear trend: states are rapidly adopting laws—33 states now regulate political deepfakes—and are now seeking to harmonize enforcement to address a fragmented landscape. While federal action remains limited to narrow areas like the Take It Down Act, and the FCC’s authority stops at telecom, state AGs are filling the gap with a unified approach. However, constitutional free-speech challenges and the difficulty of policing synthetic content without chilling legitimate speech remain real obstacles. The joint rules are a forecast grounded in current legislative momentum, not a settled policy; their final form and effectiveness are yet to be seen. What is clear is that the 2026 midterms will be the proving ground for whether coordinated state enforcement can meaningfully curb deceptive deepfake ads.
