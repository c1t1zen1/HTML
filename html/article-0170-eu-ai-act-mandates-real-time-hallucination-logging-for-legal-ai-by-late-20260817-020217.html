Why this matters now

The provided evidence confirms that the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) begins substantial enforcement on August 2, 2026, led by the European Commission's AI Office and national authorities. This date marks the activation of new transparency rules requiring AI systems to disclose their nature to users and indicate when content is AI-generated. However, the specific claim that the Act mandates 'real-time hallucination logging' for legal AI is not supported by the supplied sources. The sources describe a risk-based tiered approach, the introduction of a 'Digital Omnibus' amendment (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), and general compliance obligations for providers and deployers, but they do not detail technical specifications for error tracking or hallucination logs in the legal sector.

FIELD NOTE

Enforcement Timeline and the Digital Omnibus

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024, reaches a critical milestone on August 2, 2026, when the European Commission and national authorities begin enforcing its rules. This date is significant for the activation of General Purpose AI (GPAI) penalties and the enforcement of Article 50 transparency requirements. Recent legislative developments, specifically the 'Digital Omnibus on AI' (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), have amended the original regulations to simplify implementation and harmonize rules across the EU. This amendment, adopted in July 2026, adjusts the timeline and technical requirements, indicating a dynamic regulatory environment where compliance strategies must account for recent legislative changes.

  • Enforcement of the AI Act begins on August 2, 2026.
  • The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) amends the AI Act to simplify implementation.
  • GPAI penalties and Article 50 transparency rules activate on the enforcement date.

Transparency Obligations vs. Hallucination Logging

The sources explicitly state that from August 2026, new transparency rules require certain AI systems to inform users when they are interacting with AI and when content has been generated or altered by it. This is a disclosure obligation regarding the origin and nature of the output, rather than a technical mandate for real-time error tracking. While the Act imposes comprehensive compliance obligations on providers and deployers, including potential administrative fines and civil liability, the provided text does not mention 'hallucination logging' as a specific technical requirement. The distinction between disclosing AI involvement and logging specific factual errors is critical; the current evidence supports the former but not the latter.

  • Transparency rules require AI systems to disclose their AI nature to users.
  • Content generated or altered by AI must be identified.

Risk Tiers and Compliance Implications

The EU AI Act applies a risk-based tiered approach, where some AI uses are banned, others require conformity assessments, and most face lighter transparency obligations. For legal AI systems, the specific risk classification is not detailed in the provided snippets, but general compliance strategies must address the four risk tiers and associated deadlines. Failure to comply can result in significant administrative fines, reported in some sources as up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, as well as civil and criminal liability depending on the jurisdiction. Providers and deployers must navigate these obligations, which are enforced by the AI Office and national authorities, ensuring that their systems meet the required standards for transparency and risk management.

  • The Act uses a risk-based tiered approach to regulate AI.
  • Non-compliance can lead to fines up to €35 million or 7% of turnover.
  • Civil and criminal liability may also apply depending on jurisdiction.
