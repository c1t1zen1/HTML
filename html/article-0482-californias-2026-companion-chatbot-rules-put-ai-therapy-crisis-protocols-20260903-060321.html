California’s 2026 Companion-Chatbot Rules Put AI Therapy Crisis Protocols Under Scrutiny

California’s SB 243 makes companion-chatbot safety an operational compliance question for AI therapy: prevent harmful outputs, trigger crisis referrals, publish protocols, and disclose non-human status.

September 3, 2026 · 6:03 AM PDT · 6 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Mental Health & Therapy
AI therapymental healthchatbot safetyCalifornia regulation
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The supplied evidence indicates that California Senate Bill 243, effective January 1, 2026, establishes obligations for operators of AI companion chatbots. Those obligations include maintaining safeguards against suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content; referring users who express such content to crisis providers; disclosing that the system is not human; and, according to one source, publishing relevant safety measures. The evidence also identifies additional protections for minors, including recurring break reminders and restrictions on sexually explicit content.

For AI therapy services that fall within the law’s companion-chatbot scope, the significant issue is whether ordinary clinical-safety expectations become a separate product-compliance test. The sources support a distinct operational framework for harmful-output prevention, crisis notification, and transparency, but they do not specify clinical detection thresholds, required human review, clinician notification, emergency intervention, or validation standards. Conclusions about those elements therefore remain conditional.

Assignment angle: This angle examines whether California’s companion-chatbot safeguards create a distinct compliance test for AI therapy services’ self-harm detection, escalation, and user disclosures.

FIELD NOTE

A distinct compliance perimeter for therapy-like systems

The supplied sources describe SB 243 as regulating AI companion chatbots rather than establishing a general AI-therapy statute. The law’s relevance to an AI therapy service therefore depends on whether that service fits the companion-chatbot category; the snippets do not provide the full statutory definition or resolve that coverage question.

If covered, an AI therapy product would face obligations that are operationally distinct from merely offering therapeutic features. The operator would need a protocol designed to prevent specified harmful content, crisis-referral behavior when users express qualifying content, and disclosures that the system is not human. The evidence supports treating these as product controls rather than solely clinical-policy questions.

  • Coverage of an AI therapy service is conditional because the supplied snippets do not define the companion-chatbot category.
  • The compliance test described in the sources concerns system behavior, operator protocols, and user-facing transparency.
  • The effective date identified by the sources is January 1, 2026.

FIELD NOTE

Self-harm detection is implied, not fully specified

SB 243’s trigger is user expression of suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content. The cited legislative snippets and legal summaries connect that expression to a required notification referring the user to crisis service providers, including suicide hotlines or crisis text lines. One summary characterizes the direction as prompt.

That language creates a practical detection requirement for any covered service: the system must recognize relevant user statements reliably enough to activate the protocol. However, the supplied evidence does not state how expressions must be classified, whether indirect or ambiguous language is covered, what level of sensitivity is required, or how false positives and false negatives should be assessed. It therefore supports a compliance question about detection without proving a particular technical standard.

  • The documented trigger is a user expression involving suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm.
  • The required response includes a notification directing the user to crisis service providers.
  • The sources do not establish a mandated classifier, accuracy threshold, evaluation method, or taxonomy for risk levels.

FIELD NOTE

Escalation means crisis referral in the supplied evidence

The evidence clearly supports referral escalation: covered operators must provide notifications directing at-risk users to crisis service providers, including suicide hotlines or crisis text lines. This is more specific than a general statement that a chatbot should be safe because it ties a defined category of user expression to a prescribed user-facing action.

The snippets do not establish that the operator must alert a clinician, contact emergency services, transfer the conversation to a human, or create a clinical record. They also do not describe how the service should respond when a user rejects the referral, gives an imminent plan, or continues the conversation. Those omissions do not negate the referral obligation, but they limit what can be concluded about the full escalation pathway for AI therapy systems.

  • Crisis-provider referral is the escalation mechanism consistently identified across the supplied sources.
  • The sources do not prove a requirement for clinician handoff, emergency intervention, or human monitoring.
  • AI therapy operators may need to distinguish the statutory referral response from broader clinical escalation policies, subject to the law’s actual scope and text.

FIELD NOTE

Disclosure and published safeguards become part of the safety case

Several sources identify disclosure that the chatbot is not human as a core SB 243 requirement. For a therapy-oriented product, that disclosure matters because users may otherwise interpret a companion-style interaction as a human or clinical relationship. The supplied material also mentions suitability warnings, although it does not provide their required wording or placement.

One source states that operators must institute and publish details of harm-prevention measures and safety protocols on their websites. This makes transparency part of the compliance posture, not merely an internal engineering concern. The evidence does not specify how regulators or users will judge whether a published protocol is sufficiently detailed, nor does it establish whether disclosure alone addresses the risks of users treating the service as therapy.

  • Non-human disclosure is identified as a required safeguard in multiple summaries.
  • Published descriptions of harm-prevention measures and safety protocols are identified in the supplied evidence.
  • The evidence does not specify disclosure language, placement, usability requirements, or a clinical disclaimer standard.

↗ Where it could go

  • Users who express suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm may receive more consistent directions to crisis providers.
  • Required non-human disclosures could reduce confusion about whether a companion chatbot is a person or clinician.
  • Published safety protocols could make operators’ stated harm-prevention approach easier for users and reviewers to inspect.
  • The law may push therapy-oriented chatbot operators to test crisis-trigger behavior as an explicit product-control function rather than leaving it as an informal safety objective.

△ What could go wrong

  • A system may fail to recognize indirect, ambiguous, or context-dependent self-harm expressions because the supplied evidence provides no detection standard.
  • A crisis referral notification may be insufficient for situations requiring a broader clinical or emergency response; the sources do not define those additional pathways.
  • Users may misunderstand the limits of an AI therapy service if non-human or suitability disclosures are poorly designed, although the evidence does not specify the required presentation.
  • Operators may publish protocols without the supplied evidence establishing how completeness, effectiveness, or implementation quality will be assessed.

Closing read

Conclusion

On the supplied evidence, SB 243 creates a distinct compliance test for covered companion-chatbot services that present themselves as conversational or therapy-like systems. The test links harmful-content prevention, recognition of qualifying user expressions, crisis-provider referrals, non-human disclosure, and possibly published safety details. These requirements are distinct from a generic claim that an AI therapy product has safety features because they attach specified controls to operator conduct and user-facing behavior.

The evidence does not justify projecting a complete clinical crisis standard from SB 243 alone. It does not establish technical detection thresholds, clinician escalation, emergency response, or outcome requirements. A reasonable forecast is that covered AI therapy operators will need to show how their models activate referrals and communicate system limits, but the supplied sources do not establish how that showing will be evaluated.

Five takeaways

  • Source 1 identifies a required notification referring users who express suicidal ideation, suicide, or related content to crisis service providers.
  • Sources 3, 9, and 11 describe the operator’s obligation as a maintained prevention protocol, not merely an aspirational safety policy.
  • Sources 2, 8, and 10 identify disclosure that the AI is not human as a central safeguard.
  • Source 12 states that operators must publish details of harm-prevention measures and safety protocols, including crisis referrals.
  • Sources 1 through 12 do not establish a clinical detection threshold, mandatory clinician handoff, emergency intervention, or other therapy-specific escalation standard.
