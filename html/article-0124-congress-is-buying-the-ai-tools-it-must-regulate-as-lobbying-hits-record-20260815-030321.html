Congress Is Buying the AI Tools It Must Regulate as Lobbying Hits Records

Congress spends 88% of its visible AI budget on ChatGPT while OpenAI and Anthropic set record lobbying totals.

August 15, 2026 · 3:03 AM PDT · 6 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI Lobbying & Policy in Washington
CongressAI lobbyingprocurementoversight
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article examines a dual trend: congressional offices are spending heavily on one AI vendor—OpenAI's ChatGPT—while the same industry is pouring record sums into Washington lobbying. The evidence comes from House disbursement records reviewed by CNBC and federal lobbying disclosures. Those records show ChatGPT accounted for roughly $100,580 of at least $113,740 in identifiable House AI spending during the year ending March 31, 2026, with Anthropic's Claude a distant second at $13,160. At the same time, OpenAI nearly doubled its federal lobbying expenditure to a record $2.22 million in the first half of 2026, and Anthropic nearly tripled its spending to $3.53 million. The convergence of procurement and lobbying raises questions about how the tools Congress relies on may shape the rules it writes.

Assignment angle: It examines how congressional offices’ growing dependence on vendor AI tools intersects with record lobbying by the same industry, rather than revisiting generic campaign influence or federal regulation.

FIELD NOTE

One vendor dominates Congress's AI procurement

House disbursement records show that OpenAI's ChatGPT captured about 88% of identifiable AI dollars and roughly 96% of AI transactions in the year ending March 31, 2026. In dollar terms, ChatGPT accounted for $100,580 across 798 transactions, while Anthropic's Claude totaled $13,160 across 37 transactions. The total identifiable AI spending across House offices, committees, and institutional accounts was at least $113,740. TechCrunch reported that congressional offices rely on ChatGPT to draft memos, summarize legislation, and assist constituent communications.

  • ChatGPT: $100,580 across 798 transactions, about 88% of visible AI spending.
  • Anthropic's Claude: $13,160 across 37 transactions, a distant second.
  • Total identifiable House AI spending: at least $113,740.
  • Democrats, the loudest AI skeptics, spent the most on ChatGPT.

Lobbying expenditures hit records as AI companies court Washington

Federal lobbying disclosures show OpenAI nearly doubled its spending to a record $2.22 million in the first half of 2026 compared with the prior year. Anthropic nearly tripled its spending to $3.53 million in the same period. Quarterly data also show OpenAI spent roughly $1 million on federal lobbying in the first quarter of 2026 alone, up sharply year over year. These figures represent the highest disclosed lobbying totals for these companies, according to the Financial Times and related reporting.

  • OpenAI H1 2026 lobbying: $2.22 million, nearly double year-over-year.
  • Anthropic H1 2026 lobbying: $3.53 million, nearly triple year-over-year.
  • OpenAI Q1 2026 lobbying: roughly $1 million.
  • Both companies are increasing Washington presence as Congress debates AI regulation.

The intersection: default tools inside Congress and the rules being written

The tool that becomes the default inside Congress—currently ChatGPT at 88%—stands to influence how AI regulation itself is shaped. OpenAI's lobbying investment in Washington is paying off in market share where it matters most: the offices writing the rules. Procurement records count the charge, not what went into the window or out of it, meaning the true usage may be even broader than the spending data suggest. This creates a feedback loop: the same companies that lobby for favorable rules are also embedded in the daily workflow of the legislators drafting those rules.

  • Default AI tool usage may shape how legislators understand AI capabilities and limits.
  • Lobbying investment correlates with market share inside Congress.
  • Procurement data captures charges, not actual usage patterns.
  • Regulatory decisions may be influenced by the vendor that is already the de facto congressional assistant.

FIELD NOTE

What the spending records do and do not tell us

The disbursement records reviewed by CNBC cover House offices, committees, and institutional accounts, but they do not capture every AI purchase. Some spending may occur through agency-wide licenses or free tiers that do not appear in procurement data. The records also count charges, not actual usage, so the real volume of AI assistance inside Congress could be higher. Still, the concentration of visible spending in one vendor is striking, and the lobbying figures are disclosed under federal law, making them reliable as a floor for the industry's Washington investment.

  • Disbursement records are a partial view; free tiers and agency licenses may be excluded.
  • Procurement counts charges, not actual usage.
  • Lobbying disclosures are legally required and represent a reliable minimum.
  • The concentration in ChatGPT is based on identifiable transactions, not all possible AI spending.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence shows two clear facts: Congress spends the vast majority of its visible AI budget on ChatGPT, and OpenAI and Anthropic have set record lobbying totals in 2026. The connection between these two trends is not proven causation, but the timing and the stakes are suggestive. Congress is writing AI regulations while relying on a single vendor's tool for drafting, summarization, and constituent communications. That vendor is simultaneously spending record sums on federal lobbying. The risk is that the default tool inside Congress becomes a de facto lobbyist for its own interests, shaping how lawmakers perceive AI's benefits and dangers. The countervailing possibility is that hands-on use makes legislators better informed and more skeptical of marketing claims. What is certain is that the procurement data and lobbying disclosures will continue to be public, allowing voters and journalists to track whether the industry's Washington presence grows alongside its congressional market share.
