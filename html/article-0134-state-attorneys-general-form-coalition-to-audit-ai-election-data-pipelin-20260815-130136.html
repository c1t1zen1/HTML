Regulatory Vacuum and State-Level Enforcement

State Attorneys General are increasingly acting as primary AI enforcers due to the slow pace of federal AI legislation. Federal efforts have moved in fits and starts, leaving a regulatory vacuum that state enforcers are eager to fill. This dynamic has led to a widening of U.S. AI enforcement beyond the federal level, with states utilizing existing legal frameworks to address AI-specific concerns. For instance, the Connecticut AG issued formal guidance in February 2026 confirming that existing state laws, including anti-discrimination and consumer protection statutes, apply directly to AI systems. This legal interpretation provides a foundation for states to hold AI vendors accountable without waiting for new federal legislation, effectively turning consumer protection laws into AI governance tools.

Legal Precedents and Privilege Challenges

The legal landscape for AI investigations is evolving, with recent rulings impacting how companies interact with AI platforms. The United States v. Heppner ruling (S.D.N.Y. 2026) held that exchanges with a publicly available generative AI platform are not protected by attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine. This precedent has significant implications for legal professionals and companies using AI tools, as it removes a layer of protection for communications with AI systems. Additionally, a bipartisan coalition of 36 Attorneys General has written to Congress to oppose efforts to ban state laws that address AI, indicating a strong political will to maintain state-level regulatory authority. This pushback against federal preemption suggests that state AGs will continue to play a central role in AI oversight, potentially influencing future legislative debates.
