AI Copyright Lawsuits 2026: Supreme Court Denial Ends Thaler Appeal

Overview

The legal landscape surrounding AI-generated content reached a pivotal moment in March 2026 when the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter, effectively ending Dr. Stephen Thaler’s multi-year effort to secure copyright registration for artwork created by his AI system, the Creativity Machine. This decision affirms the longstanding position of the U.S. Copyright Office and federal courts that human authorship is a prerequisite for copyright protection under U.S. law.

The denial marks the definitive conclusion of the Thaler litigation series, which began in 2019 and became a focal point for debates over AI, creativity, and intellectual property rights. With no further appellate review available, the ruling solidifies the current legal threshold: works produced solely by artificial intelligence, without meaningful human creative input, are ineligible for copyright registration in the United States.

This outcome has significant implications for developers, artists, and businesses utilizing generative AI tools, reinforcing the necessity of human involvement in the creative process to secure legal protections for AI-assisted outputs.

Key Insights & Concepts

Human Authorship Requirement Confirmed

The Supreme Court’s denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter leaves intact the federal judiciary’s unanimous interpretation: the Copyright Act of 1976 protects only “the fruits of intellectual labor” that “are founded in the creative powers of the mind.” No current legal theory supports extending protection to works devoid of human creative contribution.

Impact on AI Developers and Users

Companies deploying generative AI tools must now assume that outputs lacking substantial human editing, selection, or arrangement carry no copyright protection. This increases legal risk for commercial use of raw AI-generated content and incentivizes workflows that integrate human curation as a prerequisite for IP rights.

Global Divergence Emerging

While the U.S. maintains a strict human-authorship standard, jurisdictions like the UK, Japan, and the EU are exploring nuanced approaches—such as attributing authorship to the person who made the arrangements necessary for the AI-generated work’s creation—creating potential conflicts in cross-border IP enforcement.

Training Data Litigation Remains Active

Although the Thaler case settled the output protection question, numerous lawsuits continue to challenge the use of copyrighted works in training AI models. These cases, involving authors, publishers, and visual artists, focus on whether unauthorized ingestion of protected works constitutes infringement, separate from the output eligibility debate.

Takeaways & Next Steps

  1. Assume no copyright protection for AI-generated outputs unless a human has made a demonstrable creative contribution—such as selecting, arranging, editing, or combining AI elements in an original way.
  2. Document human involvement in AI-assisted workflows to establish a clear chain of authorship for potential copyright registration or enforcement.
  3. Monitor ongoing training data litigation (e.g., The New York Times v. Microsoft, Getty Images v. Stability AI) as these cases may impose liability on AI providers regardless of output protection status.
  4. For international operations, assess jurisdictional differences: the UK’s “computer-generated works” provision and EU AI Act considerations may offer alternative protection pathways not available in the U.S.
  5. Engage legal counsel to review AI use cases, particularly where outputs are commercialized, to ensure compliance with evolving IP norms and mitigate infringement risk.
