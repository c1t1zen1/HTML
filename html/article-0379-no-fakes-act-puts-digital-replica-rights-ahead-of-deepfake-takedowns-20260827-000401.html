NO FAKES Act Puts Digital Replica Rights Ahead of Deepfake Takedowns

The 2026 NO FAKES proposals would make authorization, licensing, and liability central to synthetic replicas while reshaping federal and state identity rights.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The supplied evidence presents the NO FAKES Act primarily as a proposed federal right governing unauthorized digital replicas of an individual's voice or visual likeness. The House and Senate materials describe rights for individuals or right holders to authorize specified uses, while congressional hearing material describes liability for producers, companies, and platforms involved in replicas.

This article focuses on control, licensing, and liability rather than removal deadlines or disclosure labels. It draws on bill text and summaries, congressional material, sponsor statements, and legal commentary. Because the evidence covers different 2025 and 2026 versions and does not establish enactment, statutory scope and final outcomes should be treated cautiously.

FIELD NOTE

Authorization becomes the legal gate

The supplied House and Senate materials describe a federal intellectual property right protecting individuals from unauthorized digital replicas of their voice or visual likeness. The proposals give each individual or right holder the ability to authorize use of a digital replica or use of the voice or likeness for specified products and services. [Sources 3, 9, 12]

That structure places control over synthetic identity at the center of the framework. It treats a replica as a legally controlled use of a person's identity, not only as misleading content that a platform might remove. The snippets do not establish every ownership rule, remedy, exception, or evidentiary requirement, so the practical boundaries of the right remain unsettled.

  • The proposed right covers unauthorized digital replicas of voice and visual likeness.
  • Individuals or designated right holders are described as having authorization power.
  • The supplied text supports an authorization framework but does not establish the final scope of ownership.

Licenses may outlast their stated term

The 2025 House text snippet addresses authorized uses and provides that a digital replica embodied in a sound recording, image, audiovisual work, or transmission may continue to be utilized after a license expires or terminates. That provision makes license drafting consequential beyond the initial permission period. [Source 4]

For right holders and users, the important question is therefore not simply whether a replica was authorized at creation. It may also be how the license defines duration, authorized embodiments, post-expiration use, and downstream utilization. The supplied excerpt does not provide the full conditions or limitations, so it supports caution rather than a definitive interpretation of continuing-use rights.

Liability reaches production and distribution

Congressional hearing material describes two liability channels: holding individuals and companies liable for producing an unauthorized digital replica, and holding platforms liable for hosting or distributing one. This makes responsibility extend beyond the person whose identity was replicated. [Source 8]

The supplied evidence does not specify the liability threshold, available defenses, remedies, or how responsibility would be allocated among creators, commercial users, hosting services, and distributors. Still, the proposed architecture signals that synthetic-replica disputes could involve the entire chain from creation to circulation rather than only the original generator.

  • Unauthorized production is described as a potential basis for liability.
  • Hosting or distribution is separately identified as a potential liability pathway for platforms.

Federal preemption could reorder state rights

The S. 4591 text snippet states that rights established under the Act would preempt state causes of action protecting an individual's voice and visual likeness in connection with a defined digital replica in an expressive work. Legal commentary similarly describes potential preemption of certain inconsistent state laws. [Sources 2, 10]

The transition depends on enactment and on the final statutory scope. Manatt's commentary says that, while the federal proposal remains pending, the existing patchwork of state right-of-publicity laws remains available, including state laws addressing digital replicas. The evidence therefore points to a possible federal replacement or displacement in defined circumstances, not an immediate elimination of state protections. [Source 1]

  • S. 4591 contains proposed preemption language for specified expressive-work claims.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence supports a clear description of the NO FAKES proposals: they would create a federal authorization right for voice and visual likenesses, address licensing consequences, and expose producers and platforms to potential liability for unauthorized replicas. The proposed S. 4591 language would also preempt specified state-law claims involving digital replicas in expressive works.

Those are features of proposed legislation, not settled law. A possible consequence is greater leverage for right holders negotiating synthetic-replica licenses and a more uniform federal basis for claims. A possible cost is displacement of state-law pathways and increased uncertainty for businesses that create, host, or distribute replicas. Both outcomes remain projections until legislative status, final text, and implementation are established.

  • The 2025 House text contemplates continued utilization of some authorized replicas after a license expires or terminates. [Source 4]
  • Congressional material describes potential liability for both unauthorized production and platform hosting or distribution. [Source 8]
  • S. 4591 proposes preemption of specified state-law protections, but state remedies remain relevant while the federal legislation is pending. [Sources 1, 2, 10]
