The Legal Landscape: State Disclosure Mandates for AI Voice Clones

By 2026, more than 20 states have enacted political deepfake and election AI laws that include explicit disclosure requirements for AI-generated voice clones in robocalls, according to the 2026 guide from ailawsbystate.com. These laws typically require that any robocall using a synthetic voice—often defined as an AI-generated or cloned voice—must clearly state that the voice is artificial. For example, Tennessee's ELVIS Act, which went into effect in 2024, protects voice and likeness rights but also has implications for political robocalls; it requires consent for using someone's voice, and many states have built on that model.

What the Disclosure Mandates Require: Consent, Labels, and Penalties

The new mandates generally fall into two categories: consent rules and disclosure rules. Consent rules, like those in the ELVIS Act, require that a person's voice not be cloned or used without explicit written consent—especially for political purposes. Disclosure rules, meanwhile, force the campaign to label the call as AI-generated. For robocalls, this often means a spoken disclosure at the beginning of the call, such as 'This call uses an AI-generated voice.' Some states also require digital labels in caller ID or text messages accompanying the call. Penalties can include fines per violation, and in some cases, criminal penalties for fraud or election interference.

  • Consent rules: cloning a voice you have explicit written consent for is legal in every state; political use often requires additional consent (source 2).
  • Disclosure rules: explicit labels required in robocalls; failure to disclose triggers fines and legal exposure (source 5).
  • Technical safeguards: campaigns must consolidate consent and disclosure workflows, and purge data on schedule (source 4).

Uncertainty and Enforcement: What Remains Unclear

While the laws are on the books, enforcement and interpretation remain fluid. Some states have time-based bans—for example, restricting AI voice clones in the period immediately before an election—while others have permanent disclosure requirements. The distinction between 'AI-generated' and 'cloned' voices is not always clear, and some laws apply to any synthetic voice, not just those based on real people. There is also a federal patchwork: the TAKE IT DOWN Act and ELVIS Act analyses show that federal law may preempt some state rules, but as of 2026, no federal statute specifically requires disclosure in political robocalls. This creates compliance uncertainty, especially for campaigns operating across multiple states.

  • State laws vary on time-based bans vs. permanent disclosure, creating compliance complexity (source 5).
  • Federal TAKE IT DOWN Act targets non-consensual sexual deepfakes, not election robocalls (source 1).
  • No Fakes Act tracker shows ongoing legislative efforts but no federal disclosure mandate yet (source 2).

Conclusion

The evidence is clear: as of 2026, a growing majority of states require explicit disclosure of AI-generated voice clones in campaign robocalls. These mandates are not hypothetical—they are on the books and carry penalties. Campaigns that ignore them face legal exposure and reputational risk.
