California’s AI-Therapy Bill Collides With Kaiser’s Black-Box Mental-Health Triage Complaint

California’s SB 903 would require consent and clinician review for AI triage, colliding with Kaiser’s denial that its e-visit tool makes clinical decisions.

August 14, 2026 · 11:03 PM PDT · 5 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Mental Health & Therapy

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article examines a collision between two California developments: a therapists’ union complaint alleging Kaiser’s automated e-visit tool triages mental-health patients without real-time clinician review, and Senate Bill 903, which would impose disclosure, consent, and licensed-oversight requirements on AI used for triage and screening. The evidence was gathered from CalMatters, AP News, Becker’s Hospital Review, Lawire, and related outlets. The focus is on automated intake and referral decisions inside a healthcare system, not consumer chatbot conversations or model safety audits.

FIELD NOTE

Kaiser’s black-box triage complaint

A therapists’ union has filed a complaint alleging that Kaiser’s e-visit tool screens mental-health patients and issues care recommendations without real-time clinician review, which the union says violates state law. Kaiser responded to CalMatters that its e-visit tool “does not use AI to diagnose patients, make clinical decisions, or determine medical necessity.” The complaint centers on automated intake and referral decisions inside a large health plan, rather than on chatbot therapy marketed to consumers.

  • Union alleges Kaiser’s algorithm, not clinicians, is triaging mental-health patients.
  • Kaiser denies the tool makes diagnoses or clinical decisions.
  • The complaint highlights a gap between automated triage and human oversight.

FIELD NOTE

What SB 903 actually targets

Senate Bill 903 passed the California Senate 39-0 on May 19, 2026. The proposal would generally limit AI in psychotherapy to administrative or supplementary support. It would require disclosure and patient consent before AI is used for recording, transcription, triage, or screening. Companion chatbots could not be marketed or presented as providing psychotherapy services. The bill would also prohibit AI from making therapeutic decisions without the review of a licensed professional.

  • SB 903 passed the Senate unanimously on May 19, 2026.
  • AI triage and screening would require disclosure and patient consent.
  • Companion chatbots cannot be advertised as psychotherapy.
  • Licensed human review is required for therapeutic decisions.

The collision: consent and clinician review vs. Kaiser’s denial

SB 903’s triage provisions directly address the Kaiser complaint. Kaiser says its e-visit tool does not use AI to diagnose or determine medical necessity, but the union alleges the tool screens patients and issues care recommendations. Under SB 903, AI triage would require disclosure and consent, and therapeutic decisions would need licensed human oversight. Kaiser’s denial may not shield it if the tool is performing triage or screening functions that the bill defines as requiring transparency.

  • The bill’s triage rules overlap with the union’s allegations.
  • Kaiser’s denial focuses on diagnosis and medical necessity, not triage or screening.

What’s next in Sacramento

As of August 11, 2026, the bill’s status remains pending in the California Legislature after passing the Senate. The measure would need Assembly approval and the governor’s signature to become law. The outcome will determine whether AI triage in health systems requires explicit patient consent and human oversight, and whether Kaiser’s current e-visit tool would need to change.

  • SB 903 passed the Senate but is not yet law.
  • Assembly and governor action are still required.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence shows a clear factual collision: a union complaint alleges Kaiser’s e-visit tool performs automated triage and care recommendations without real-time clinician review, while Kaiser maintains the tool does not diagnose or make clinical decisions. SB 903 would impose consent and disclosure requirements for AI triage and screening, and would require licensed professional review for therapeutic decisions. Whether Kaiser’s tool falls within the bill’s definition of triage or screening is not yet settled, and the bill itself has not become law. Projections about Kaiser changing its system or the bill passing remain speculative until the Assembly votes and the governor acts.
