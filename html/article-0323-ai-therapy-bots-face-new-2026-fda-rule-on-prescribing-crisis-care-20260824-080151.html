AI Therapy Bots Face New 2026 FDA Rule on Prescribing Crisis Care

FDA’s 2026 rulemaking targets AI therapy chatbots’ crisis-intervention advice, a first regulatory milestone beyond privacy and efficacy debates.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article focuses on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s first rulemaking specifically aimed at AI therapy chatbots’ ability to recommend crisis interventions—such as when a chatbot suggests a user call 988, go to an emergency room, or take other urgent actions. Unlike earlier debates centered on data privacy or general therapeutic efficacy, this new regulatory milestone addresses a narrower, higher-stakes function: the chatbot’s role as a crisis-care recommender. FIELD NOTE

What the 2026 FDA Rule Targets

The FDA’s Digital Health Advisory Committee met on November 6, 2025, to examine generative-AI mental health devices, including a hypothetical prescription-only LLM-based chatbot for major depressive disorder. The committee systematically catalogued failure modes, including the chatbot’s potential to recommend inappropriate crisis interventions. This meeting set the stage for the FDA’s first rulemaking specifically addressing AI therapy chatbots’ crisis-care recommendations—a distinct regulatory action from generic privacy or efficacy rulemaking. The rule’s focus is on whether a chatbot can legally suggest crisis actions like calling a hotline, presenting at an emergency department, or involving emergency services, and under what conditions such suggestions must be prescribed or overseen by a human clinician.

The Regulatory Boundary: Prescription vs. Wellness

A key regulatory question is where the FDA draws the line between a prescription-only medical device and a wellness product. Structured cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) bots with fixed content can sometimes pursue a 510(k) pathway by referencing predicate digital therapeutics. Open-ended generative chatbots typically remain in wellness territory or partner with health systems under analysis protocols. The new rulemaking appears to be a boundary exercise: it will clarify when a chatbot’s crisis-intervention advice crosses from wellness into medical-device territory, requiring FDA authorization and a prescription by a healthcare provider. The FDA’s stated scope for its Digital Health Advisory Committee focuses on novel generative-AI digital mental health medical devices, and the agency has noted that many AI mental health apps are already on the market operating without regulation.

State-Level Parallels and the Push for Disclosure

While the FDA moves on the federal front, states have enacted their own chatbot laws. California’s law, effective January 1, 2026, requires chatbots to disclose they are not licensed providers, detect mental health crises and suicidal ideation, and establish guardrails for users under 18. Illinois’ law, effective August 1, 2025, prohibits chatbots from representing themselves as licensed providers. Texas requires clinician disclosure, effective January 1, 2026. Many 2026 state bills mirror these three enacted laws, showing a coordinated push. The FDA’s federal rulemaking on crisis-care recommendations will interact with these state disclosure and crisis-detection requirements, creating a layered regulatory environment for AI therapy bots.

  • California’s law (effective 1/1/26) requires crisis and suicidal ideation detection and guardrails for minors.

Legal and Ethical Pressure for FDA Action

Legal commentators have argued that health AI chatbots are legally medical devices and that the FDA should treat them as such. Without restrictive design standards, the FDA should be obligated to take action against products like “ChatGPT Health” as unregulated medical devices. This pressure comes from the high risk of incorrect or misleading medical advice, especially in crisis situations. The FDA’s 2026 rulemaking on crisis-care recommendations is a direct response to this legal and ethical pressure, focusing on the highest-risk function of AI therapy chatbots: advising urgent care. The rule is expected to require human oversight for any chatbot that recommends crisis interventions, aligning with the FDA’s broader risk-management framework for high-impact AI.

  • Without clear guidance, chatbots may avoid crisis-related conversations entirely, worsening outcomes for users in need.
