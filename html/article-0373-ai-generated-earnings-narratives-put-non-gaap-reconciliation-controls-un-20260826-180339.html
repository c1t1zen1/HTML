AI-Generated Earnings Narratives Put Non-GAAP Reconciliation Controls Under 2026 Audit Pressure

AI can draft earnings narratives quickly, but 2026 audit pressure centers on whether finance teams can trace every non-GAAP claim to audited GAAP results and reconciliation evidence.

August 26, 2026 · 6:03 PM PDT · 7 min read · 12 sources · Original family: Generative AI for Financial Reporting

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The supplied evidence points to a narrow control question: can finance teams demonstrate that an AI-generated earnings narrative faithfully connects non-GAAP measures to their most directly comparable GAAP measures? The issue is not simply whether generative AI improves reporting speed. It is whether teams can preserve reconciliation logic, identify exceptions, document review, and prevent probabilistic output from becoming unsupported disclosure.

The evidence was gathered from secondary commentary, company earnings materials, and an SEC filing exhibit. Company examples from Microsoft and Blackbaud show reconciliations included in earnings communications, while the Arm exhibit shows a case in which projected non-GAAP reconciliations were not provided because they could not be prepared without unreasonable effort. Commentary on COSO, PCAOB priorities, and SEC requirements is treated cautiously because the supplied material does not include the underlying regulatory documents.

Assignment angle: This angle examines whether finance teams can prove that AI-drafted earnings narratives accurately reconcile non-GAAP metrics with audited results, rather than treating generative AI as a generic reporting-efficiency tool.

FIELD NOTE

The control question is traceability, not drafting speed

The supplied evidence describes AI outputs as probabilistic and warns that set-and-forget assurance is inadequate. That changes the control objective for earnings narratives: finance teams need evidence that the generated language was grounded in approved figures, reviewed for accuracy, and kept aligned with the underlying reconciliation.

One secondary source proposes continuous control evidence covering all transactions, with auditors reviewing exception reports instead of relying only on manual samples. That is presented as a potential operating model, not as an established audit requirement. The practical test remains whether evidence is complete and reviewable when a narrative makes a claim about adjusted earnings, operating expenses, or another non-GAAP measure.

  • AI drafting does not eliminate management responsibility for the accuracy of reported measures.
  • Probabilistic output requires monitoring rather than one-time assurance.
  • Continuous evidence could help, but only if it shows how narrative claims connect to controlled financial inputs.

FIELD NOTE

Non-GAAP reconciliation rules still govern the published narrative

The supplied material describes Regulation G as applying to non-GAAP measures released outside formal filings, including earnings press releases furnished under Form 8-K Item 2.02. It characterizes the obligation as including reconciliation to the comparable GAAP measure and a general anti-fraud principle that the non-GAAP measure must not be misleading. The evidence also identifies SEC Item 10(e), Regulation G, and related compliance guidance as the relevant framework, although the detailed requirements are not reproduced in the snippets.

Company materials illustrate the expected disclosure pattern. Microsoft states that its non-GAAP information is intended to help investors understand performance and should not replace or be considered superior to GAAP measures, while also providing a constant-currency reconciliation. Blackbaud states that reconciliation to the most directly comparable GAAP measures appears in the financial statement tables included in its release.

  • An AI-generated narrative must not obscure the comparable GAAP measure or the reconciliation supporting the claim.
  • Boilerplate that non-GAAP measures are not substitutes for GAAP does not, by itself, prove numerical accuracy.
  • Reconciliation tables provide an observable anchor against which generated narrative language can be checked.

FIELD NOTE

Forward-looking measures expose the hardest exception

The SEC exhibit supplied for Arm states that reconciliations for projected non-GAAP operating expense and projected non-GAAP fully diluted earnings per share were not provided because Arm could not provide them without unreasonable effort. The exhibit identifies those measures as forward-looking non-GAAP financial measures. This is a concrete example of a reconciliation limitation, not evidence that AI caused the limitation.

For AI-assisted reporting, the control risk is that a model may draft confident explanatory language around a projected measure even when the underlying reconciliation is unavailable or materially constrained. The evidence does not establish a specific AI rule for this situation. It does support treating missing, partial, or effort-constrained reconciliations as exceptions requiring explicit finance review rather than allowing automated narrative completion.

  • Forward-looking non-GAAP measures can present reconciliation constraints that differ from historical reporting.
  • An inability to provide a reconciliation should be visible to the review process before narrative generation is finalized.
  • Confident wording must not imply support that the underlying disclosure record does not contain.

FIELD NOTE

Audit pressure may arrive before dedicated AI disclosure rules

The supplied commentary reports that PCAOB inspection priorities scrutinize AI in audited reporting and that AS 2201 and AS 2101 are described as taking effect for fiscal years beginning on or after December 15, 2026. Because this timing and interpretation come from a secondary source snippet, they should be verified against primary PCAOB material before being used for compliance conclusions.

Other supplied commentary states that, as of mid-2026, no dedicated FASB or standalone SEC AI disclosure standard exists and that existing frameworks remain the relevant reference point. The evidence therefore suggests a regulatory gap may coexist with rising expectations for ordinary internal control, reconciliation, anti-fraud, and auditability disciplines. That combination is a projection about pressure and implementation, not proof of a new AI-specific rule.

  • Absence of a standalone AI disclosure rule does not establish absence of control obligations.
  • Secondary descriptions of PCAOB priorities and 2026 effective dates require primary-source confirmation.
  • Finance teams should expect scrutiny to focus on evidence supporting reported numbers and narrative claims.

  • Set-and-forget assurance may fail to detect changes or errors in probabilistic AI outputs (Sources 1 and 12).
  • A non-GAAP narrative can become misleading if it is not reconciled to the comparable GAAP measure or if the reconciliation is obscured (Source 4).
  • AI may generate confident language for projected measures whose reconciliation is unavailable or constrained by unreasonable effort (Source 5).

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports several facts: non-GAAP disclosures remain tied to comparable-GAAP reconciliation requirements; company earnings materials commonly present reconciliation information; and at least one SEC exhibit describes projected non-GAAP measures for which reconciliation was not provided because of unreasonable effort. The evidence also describes AI outputs as probabilistic and argues for ongoing monitoring rather than set-and-forget assurance.

The forward-looking conclusion is narrower. If finance teams use AI to draft earnings narratives, audit pressure is likely to concentrate on whether they can reproduce the path from approved GAAP and non-GAAP inputs to final language, including exceptions and reviewer decisions. The supplied material does not prove a new AI-specific SEC disclosure regime, so the defensible posture is to strengthen evidence under existing reporting and control frameworks while monitoring primary regulatory developments.

Five takeaways

  • The central 2026 control issue is proving that AI-drafted narrative claims trace to approved GAAP and non-GAAP data, not merely proving that a model produced text (Sources 1, 2, and 12).
  • Regulation G and SEC Item 10(e) remain the supplied evidence's core non-GAAP reference points, including reconciliation and anti-misleading considerations (Sources 3 and 4).
  • Microsoft and Blackbaud show that reconciliation information is a visible feature of company earnings communications, giving reviewers material against which AI narratives can be checked (Sources 6 and 9).
  • Arm's SEC exhibit demonstrates that forward-looking non-GAAP reconciliation can be unavailable because of unreasonable effort, creating an exception that automated drafting must not conceal (Source 5).
  • The supplied evidence describes increasing AI-related control attention but does not establish a standalone SEC or FASB AI disclosure rule; primary-source verification remains necessary (Sources 1, 7, and 10).
