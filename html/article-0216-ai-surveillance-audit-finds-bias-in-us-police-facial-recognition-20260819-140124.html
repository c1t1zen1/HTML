Why Bias Persists in Police Facial Recognition

The bias is encoded in the technology itself. The SAGE journal analysis explains that facial recognition operates by comparing a probe image against a database of known faces, and the promise of rapid, accurate identification is undermined by uneven performance across skin tones, ages, and other attributes. The FAS report notes that technical fixes alone cannot fully resolve the bias because the underlying data and evaluation metrics are skewed. Police leaders admit the technology carries inherent bias, as shown by a Guardian report quoting a police AI chief who acknowledged the discovery of in-built bias in a national database's retrospective facial recognition system.

  • SAGE journal's case study describes facial recognition as 'a case of encoded bias,' linking technical design to social outcomes.
  • The Guardian report quotes APCC forensic science lead Darryl Preston, who called for independent oversight after bias was found in a police national database.
  • FAS report stresses that technical fixes have limits, because bias is embedded in data collection and benchmarking practices.

From Audit to Action: Oversight and Regulation

The audit has intensified calls for independent oversight and regulation. Privacy International notes that from August 2026, retrospective use of facial recognition will face additional conditions and safeguards under the EU AI Act, including judicial or administrative authority approval. In the U.S., however, rules lag behind adoption, as Stateline reports: police use of AI grows while laws remain incomplete, allowing bias to continue without accountability. The ACLUM report warns that AI-powered surveillance systems, including facial recognition, can track individuals across daily life, amplifying the stakes of biased errors.

  • Privacy International highlights that EU AI Act rules starting August 2026 will impose safeguards on retrospective FRT searches.
  • Stateline reports that U.S. police AI adoption outpaces rulemaking, leaving bias unchecked.
  • ACLUM warns that integrated surveillance suites with facial recognition enable continuous tracking, increasing the harm of any single biased match.

Public Trust and the Knowledge Gap

Public attitudes toward police facial recognition are shaped by awareness of its flaws. A ScienceDirect study found that deeper general AI knowledge was linked with decreased trust and support for facial recognition in policing. This suggests that technological literacy exposes algorithmic limitations and ethical concerns, including bias, privacy, and surveillance capacity. The audit's findings may further erode public confidence, especially as wrongful arrests become more visible in media and legal cases.

  • ScienceDirect study shows that higher AI knowledge reduces support for police FRT, contrary to assumptions that familiarity breeds acceptance.
  • Participants expressed significant concerns about algorithmic bias, privacy, and surveillance capabilities.
  • This knowledge effect implies that audits and public reporting on bias can shift attitudes toward stricter oversight.
