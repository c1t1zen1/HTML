Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The regulatory landscape for the 2026 U.S. midterms is defined by a significant disconnect between the rapid adoption of AI in political advertising and the operational capacity of the Federal Election Commission (FEC). While campaigns are leveraging AI-optimized messaging and voter files containing hundreds of data points to execute real-time micro-targeting, the FEC has been unable to conduct major enforcement actions or rulemaking due to a lack of seated commissioners since May 2025. This structural deficit leaves the agency in a state of administrative stagnation, limiting its ability to address novel issues like algorithmic targeting or deceptive AI content.

The evidence gathered indicates that while there is political pressure for the FEC to initiate rulemaking on AI-produced content, the agency currently lacks the legal authority to act. Nominations for new commissioners have been submitted, but the timeline for confirmation and seating remains uncertain, potentially leaving the midterms without a functional federal referee. This creates a regulatory vacuum where billions of dollars in political advertising are deployed with minimal federal oversight regarding the use of AI technologies.

FIELD NOTE

The FEC's Operational Paralysis

The Federal Election Commission has been effectively out of commission for a significant portion of the 2026 election cycle. Since May 1, 2025, the agency has lacked the number of commissioners required for a quorum, leaving it unable to perform functions beyond publishing basic financial disclosure reports. Major enforcement actions, including audits, fines, and rulemaking, require at least four commissioners to vote, a threshold the agency cannot currently meet. As of the 2026 midterm election cycle, only two commissioners are seated, rendering the FEC incapable of investigating potential violations of federal campaign finance law.

This paralysis has resulted in a growing backlog of cases with no clear end in sight. The agency's inability to act is exacerbated by the timing of new nominations, which, even if confirmed, could take weeks or months for new members to take their positions. Consequently, the FEC is unable to serve as a referee in a midterm cycle where billions of dollars have already been spent, creating a period of zero federal oversight for potential legal infractions.

Regulatory Pressure and Rulemaking Calls

Despite the FEC's operational limitations, there is significant pressure from legal experts and lawmakers for the agency to address AI-specific issues. The Brennan Center for Justice has called on the FEC to initiate a rulemaking process specifically targeting deliberately deceptive AI-produced campaign content. This call reflects a broader concern that current regulations are insufficient to handle the nuances of AI-generated media and algorithmic targeting.

Lawmakers have reinforced this pressure, urging the FEC to act before the midterms conclude. However, the agency's inability to reach a quorum means that any rulemaking process is currently stalled. The Campaign Legal Center has also filed complaints alleging that super PACs are using complex structures to obscure funders, adding to the list of unresolved enforcement issues. The disconnect between the call for regulatory action and the FEC's capacity to deliver it is a central feature of the 2026 election landscape.

Broader Election Integrity Concerns

The regulatory vacuum at the FEC is part of a broader pattern of weakened federal oversight in the 2026 midterms. In July 2026, the Election Assistance Commission, a bipartisan agency that certifies voting equipment and administers federal election funding, was left without a quorum after the firing of its three remaining commissioners. This action occurred months before the midterms, raising concerns about the integrity of the voting process itself.

Watchdog reports indicate that the 2026 midterms are wide open to foreign interference due to these institutional weaknesses. The simultaneous defunding or defanging of key election oversight bodies creates a compounded risk for election security. While the FEC's specific role is limited to campaign finance, its inaction contributes to a general environment of reduced federal scrutiny, which may embolden the use of unregulated AI technologies in political campaigns.
