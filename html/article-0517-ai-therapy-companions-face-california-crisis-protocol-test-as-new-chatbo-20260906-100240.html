AI Therapy Companions Face California Crisis-Protocol Test as New Chatbot Rules Take Effect

California’s SB 243 sets a crisis-response baseline for AI companions, testing whether self-harm interactions receive stronger safeguards than ordinary wellness-app conversations.

September 6, 2026 · 10:02 AM PDT · 7 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Mental Health & Therapy
AI therapychatbot safetyCalifornia law
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

California SB 243 took effect January 1, 2026, according to legal and regulatory summaries in the supplied evidence. Its central crisis provision is broader than a simple disclosure: the bill text says operators must maintain a protocol intended to prevent suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content from being produced to users, including referral notifications to crisis service providers when users express such content.

This analysis prioritizes the Legislature’s bill text and uses legal analyses, legislative trackers, and regulatory summaries for context. The evidence supports a clear distinction between mandatory companion-chatbot crisis protocols and general transparency or suitability notices, but it does not establish that every wellness app is legally outside SB 243 or specify how regulators will evaluate real-world performance.

Assignment angle: This angle examines whether California’s 2026 SB 243 requirements force companion chatbots to handle self-harm and crisis interactions differently from ordinary wellness apps.

FIELD NOTE

The crisis obligation goes beyond an AI label

The strongest evidence is the statutory language in Source 2. An operator cannot allow a companion chatbot to engage with users unless the operator maintains a protocol for preventing the production of suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content. The provision also identifies a required response when a user expresses such content: a notification referring the user to crisis service providers, including a suicide hotline or crisis text line.

That structure creates a compliance test focused on behavior during a high-risk interaction. A notice that a system is non-human, or a general warning about suitability, addresses transparency and user expectations; it does not by itself demonstrate the crisis protocol described in the bill. Sources 1, 10, and 12 independently describe disclosure and safety-protocol obligations, reinforcing that these are separate compliance elements.

  • The quoted bill text requires a maintained protocol before the companion chatbot engages with users.
  • The protocol must address suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content.
  • A user expressing such content must receive a notification referring them to crisis providers, including a hotline or crisis text line.
  • AI disclosure and suitability warnings appear alongside, not instead of, crisis-response requirements.

FIELD NOTE

Why companion chatbots face a different test than wellness apps

The supplied sources frame SB 243 around companion chatbot platforms rather than wellness software generally. Sources 1 and 5 describe the law as targeting companion chatbots and identify crisis-prevention protocols as a specific requirement. Sources 4, 7, and 10 place the effective date at January 1, 2026.

The practical distinction is therefore the type of interaction the law addresses. A companion chatbot designed for ongoing engagement may need a defined crisis pathway that changes how it responds to self-harm or suicide-related expressions. The supplied evidence does not provide a complete legal definition of an ordinary wellness app, however, so it would be too broad to conclude that wellness apps categorically avoid the same duties.

  • SB 243 is described as regulating companion chatbot platforms.
  • The evidence identifies crisis protocols as a mandatory companion-chatbot obligation.
  • The material does not establish a categorical exemption for ordinary wellness apps.
  • The key difference is whether a platform has the covered companion-chatbot function, not merely whether it markets itself as wellness software.

FIELD NOTE

Implementation will determine whether the rule changes conversations

The law’s apparent operational effect is a shift from informal safety intentions to documented response behavior. Platforms may need to detect relevant expressions, prevent prohibited content from being produced, and issue crisis-service referrals. The snippets do not explain the required detection threshold, timing, wording, escalation path, or treatment of ambiguous statements.

Other obligations add layers around the crisis response. Source 1 identifies AI disclosure, suitability warnings, recurring break reminders for minors, and content restrictions. Source 8 likewise describes special protections for minors, while Source 3 says audit and reporting records begin July 1, 2027. These provisions suggest that implementation will involve both immediate user-facing safeguards and later accountability processes, but the supplied material does not show how compliance will be measured in practice.

  • A functioning protocol likely requires more than a static warning, because the bill text ties the response to user expressions of self-harm or suicide.
  • The evidence does not specify detection thresholds, response timing, or escalation procedures.
  • Minor protections include recurring breaks and content restrictions in the supplied summaries.
  • Source 3 identifies July 1, 2027, as the start of specified audit and reporting records.

FIELD NOTE

The unresolved test is reliability under pressure

The supplied evidence establishes a regulatory baseline, but not whether current systems meet it consistently. A platform could disclose that it is artificial and still fail to prevent harmful content or provide an appropriate crisis referral. Conversely, a platform could trigger a referral in a borderline conversation without the evidence explaining whether that would satisfy the law or create other user-experience problems.

Future policy may expand beyond California’s approach. Source 9 forecasts that later laws could add youth-specific provisions or connect chatbot oversight to age assurance and data-protection frameworks. That is a forecast, not an enacted requirement shown in the supplied material. Readers should distinguish those projected developments from SB 243’s stated effective date and crisis-protocol language.

  • The evidence does not state how regulators will test or enforce protocol quality.

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports a factual bottom line: effective January 1, 2026, California SB 243 establishes companion-chatbot obligations that include maintaining a protocol to prevent suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content and referring users who express such content to crisis service providers. Disclosure, suitability warnings, and minor protections form additional parts of the described framework.

The projection is that companion platforms will need to treat crisis conversations differently from ordinary wellness interactions, with dedicated detection and referral behavior rather than only general product disclaimers. Whether that distinction produces safer outcomes remains unproven here. The decisive signals will be the quality of protocols, actual responses to self-harm expressions, and the later audit and reporting record.

Five takeaways

  • SB 243 became effective January 1, 2026, according to multiple supplied legal summaries (Sources 4, 7, 10).
  • The bill text conditions companion-chatbot engagement on maintaining a protocol addressing suicidal ideation, suicide, and self-harm content (Source 2).
  • The stated crisis response includes a notification referring users to crisis service providers, including a suicide hotline or crisis text line (Source 2).
  • The supplied evidence describes additional transparency, suitability, minor-protection, and content-related duties beyond crisis referrals (Sources 1, 8, 10, 12).
  • The evidence supports a regulatory distinction for covered companion chatbots, but does not fully define how that distinction applies to every ordinary wellness app (Sources 1, 2, 5).
