AI Video Models Face Data-Center Power Test as Hyperscalers Expand Generative Compute

AI video’s next constraint may be physical: hyperscalers are expanding GPUs, power contracts, cooling capacity, and chip supply while forecasts diverge on the scale of the squeeze.

September 13, 2026 · 7:04 AM PDT · 7 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI Video Generation Models

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This synthesis uses only the supplied search material. It combines analyst forecasts, industry reporting, a reported corporate power agreement, and source snippets describing data-center conditions. Several figures are projections rather than established outcomes, the estimates use different definitions, and the supplied evidence does not provide per-video energy use, cooling load, or accelerator-utilization measurements.

Assignment angle: This shifts coverage from legal safeguards to the infrastructure bottleneck—electricity, cooling, and accelerator capacity required to serve increasingly long video generations.

FIELD NOTE

Capital is scaling faster than physical capacity

Analysts cited by CarbonCredits expect the major cloud companies to spend more than $600 billion in capital expenditures during 2026, a projected 36% increase from 2025.[1] Axis Intelligence gives a higher estimate, saying the Big Five hyperscalers will spend $725 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 and face a projected 9.3 GW U.S. capacity shortfall.[7] These numbers are forecasts with different scopes, so they should not be treated as one settled industry total.

The sources nevertheless converge on a physical bottleneck. Datacenters.com says power availability has become the primary constraint on data-center development, while GPUInsights identifies megawatts, interconnection queues, and delivering electricity to racks as limiting factors.[6][8] GigeNET similarly describes power and thermal management as hard limits. AWS’s plan to add more than one million Nvidia GPUs from 2026, combined with reported demand exceeding its expectations, suggests that accelerator procurement alone may not remove the deployment constraint.[10][12]

  • The supplied 2026 spending estimates range from more than $600 billion in total hyperscaler capital expenditure to $725 billion in AI infrastructure.
  • Multiple sources identify electricity availability and interconnection as infrastructure bottlenecks.
  • GPU purchasing plans can outpace the power and thermal capacity required to operate the hardware.

FIELD NOTE

Power procurement becomes part of model capacity

The supplied evidence includes a concrete example of hyperscaler power strategy: in January 2026, Meta reportedly entered a 20-year power-purchase agreement with Vistra for more than 2,600 MW of zero-carbon energy from three nuclear plants.[2] The agreement indicates that securing electricity can be treated as a long-term capacity decision rather than a routine operating expense, although the snippet does not establish how much of that power will support video workloads or when all capacity will be available.

The infrastructure challenge also extends beyond generation. GPUInsights points to interconnection queues, and GigeNET emphasizes cooling and thermal management alongside electricity.[6][10] For longer video generations, the relevant operational question is whether sufficient power and cooling exist at the accelerator rack when inference demand arrives. The supplied material supports that concern directionally, but it does not quantify the energy or cooling requirement of a single generation.

  • Meta’s reported agreement covers more than 2,600 MW of zero-carbon energy over 20 years.
  • Interconnection timing can matter as much as nominal electricity supply.
  • Longer video workloads require rack-level power and cooling capacity, but the supplied sources provide no per-generation measurements.

FIELD NOTE

Accelerator and chip supply remain part of the test

Power is not the only capacity question. DatacenterKnowledge reports that AWS had planned to add more than one million Nvidia GPUs beginning in 2026 and that customer demand later exceeded those expectations.[12] That report links model demand to a concrete hardware scale-up, but the supplied snippet does not state how many GPUs were ultimately installed or how many are dedicated to video generation.

The supply chain is also expanding. DatacenterKnowledge says TSMC is adding fabs and packaging capacity in Arizona, ramping 2 nm alongside 3 nm and 5 nm, and targeting GPUs, CPUs, networking silicon, and custom AI accelerators.[9] EnkiAI projects the global data-center GPU market at $28.6 billion in 2026 and $183.0 billion by 2034, with a 14.2% CAGR.[4] Those market figures are projections, not proof that supply will match demand, but they reinforce the scale of the expected buildout.

  • Reported customer demand exceeded AWS’s earlier expectations.
  • TSMC’s described Arizona expansion targets GPU, networking, and custom-accelerator supply.
  • The GPU market figures supplied by EnkiAI are forecasts rather than realized results.

The evidence supports a bottleneck thesis, not a precise video cost model

The strongest shared signal is qualitative: several supplied sources describe power, location, interconnection, or cooling as constraints on AI expansion.[6][8][10] That supports a shift in coverage from model safeguards alone toward the infrastructure required to serve models at scale. It does not establish that electricity is the only bottleneck, nor does it prove that every video provider faces the same constraint.

The evidence also has clear gaps. Source 3 provides no snippet, so its headline cannot substantiate a 95% GPU-idle claim. Source 5 provides a generic Reuters energy-news description rather than substantive evidence from the linked article. None of the supplied snippets reports video-generation energy per clip, rack power density, cooling-water use, accelerator utilization, or service latency. Those measurements are necessary to turn the infrastructure warning into a workload-specific cost model.

  • The supplied evidence is sufficient for a directional infrastructure thesis.
  • It is insufficient for calculating the electricity or cooling cost of a video generation.

Closing read

Conclusion

The directly reported developments are substantial: hyperscalers are planning very large GPU and infrastructure expansions, AWS has reportedly seen demand exceed an earlier GPU plan, Meta has reportedly secured more than 2,600 MW through a long-term zero-carbon power agreement, and TSMC is expanding semiconductor and packaging capacity.[2][9][12] Multiple supplied sources also place power availability, interconnection, and cooling at the center of the data-center challenge.[6][8][10]

The scale of the future squeeze remains less certain. The more-than-$600 billion, $725 billion, $28.6 billion, $183.0 billion, 14.2%, 9.3 GW, and 20%-by-2035 figures are forecasts or projected estimates, and they do not share one definition or measurement method.[1][4][7][11] The defensible conclusion is therefore directional: AI video expansion will increasingly depend on synchronized accelerator supply, electricity procurement, grid access, and thermal design. The supplied evidence does not yet show exactly how much power a longer video generation requires or whether planned infrastructure will be delivered on schedule.
