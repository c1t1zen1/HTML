Verification Duties and the Rule 11 Burden

Courts are clarifying that generative AI changes a lawyer's workflow, not their ethical duties of competence, candor, and verification. The Sixth and Seventh Circuits have both agreed on this principle, though they diverge on sanctions. In Mississippi federal court, Judge Aycock sanctioned four lawyers for AI hallucinated cases, explicitly linking the conduct to Rule 11. The message is that a lawyer must verify every citation against a primary source — Westlaw, Lexis, a court's official records, or a citation-backed legal AI platform — before signing a filing.

  • Sixth and Seventh Circuit cases agree AI changes workflow, not ethical duties.
  • Judge Aycock sanctioned four lawyers in Mississippi federal court under Rule 11.
  • Verification must be against a primary source, not general-purpose AI output.

FIELD NOTE

Disclosure Rules: A Patchwork, Not a Uniform Mandate

AI disclosure requirements vary sharply by jurisdiction. Many courts now require attorneys to disclose AI use in legal filings, and 35+ state bars have issued guidance or requirements. But the Illinois Supreme Court released guidance stating that judges should refrain from requiring attorney disclosure of AI use, believing the Rules of Professional Conduct already cover the issue. This patchwork means lawyers must check each court's standing orders and local rules, because a filing that is compliant in one district may be sanctionable in another.

Practical Compliance: Cite-Check Logs and Primary-Source Verification

Compliance guidance now centers on a simple but strict workflow: never use general-purpose AI (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) as your primary legal research tool, because these are text generators, not legal databases. Lawyers should verify every citation against a primary source and maintain a cite-check log that tracks each AI-generated citation through verification. Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence shows federal courts are converging on a core principle: AI changes workflow, not the lawyer's duty to verify. The Sixth and Seventh Circuits articulate this explicitly, while district courts like Judge Aycock's enforce it through Rule 11 sanctions. Disclosure rules remain fragmented — some jurisdictions require explicit AI-use statements, while Illinois tells judges to refrain — so lawyers must navigate a patchwork of local standing orders.

Five takeaways

  • Federal courts are imposing filing-level controls, including cancelled trials and barred lawyers, for AI-fabricated case law.
  • The Seventh Circuit now requires attorneys to verify AI-generated legal citations, and the Sixth Circuit agrees AI does not change ethical duties.
  • Rule 11 sanctions are being applied to AI hallucinated citations, as seen in Judge Aycock's Mississippi ruling.
  • AI disclosure rules are not uniform: Illinois tells judges to refrain from requiring disclosure, while 35+ state bars have issued requirements.
  • Compliance requires verifying every citation against a primary source and maintaining a cite-check log, not just disclosing AI use.
