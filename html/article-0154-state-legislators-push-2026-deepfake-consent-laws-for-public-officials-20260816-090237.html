State Legislators Push 2026 Deepfake Consent Laws for Public Officials

State legislatures are accelerating deepfake consent laws for public officials in 2026, creating a patchwork of regulations distinct from federal frameworks and targeting election integrity and nonconsensual imagery.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The regulatory landscape for synthetic media is shifting rapidly at the state level, with a significant surge in legislation between 2024 and 2025. Ballotpedia’s data indicates that 82% of the 174 deepfake laws approved by states since 2019 were enacted during this two-year period, signaling an explosive growth in state-level regulation. This wave of legislation is driven by concerns over election disinformation, nonconsensual sexual content, and the harassment of public figures, with bipartisan backing often cited in legislative efforts.

The evidence gathered from multiple trackers and legal analyses highlights a fragmented approach where states are addressing specific harms rather than adopting broad AI governance frameworks. While federal bills like S.1837 remain pending, state statutes are codifying disclosure duties and penalties for synthetic media. The focus is increasingly on protecting children and regulating political campaigns, with 33 states now specifically regulating deepfakes in this context. This state-by-state divergence creates a complex compliance environment for creators and platforms, necessitating a detailed understanding of local statutes.

FIELD NOTE

The 2024-2025 Legislative Surge

State legislatures have demonstrated an unprecedented pace in enacting deepfake laws, with a striking concentration of activity in recent years. According to Ballotpedia, 82% of all state deepfake laws approved since 2019 were passed between 2024 and 2025. This rapid expansion reflects a growing legislative consensus on the need to address the unique risks posed by synthetic media, particularly in the lead-up to major election cycles.

The momentum continues into 2026, with every state except Ohio and New Mexico now addressing sexually explicit deepfakes in some form. This brings the total to 48 states regulating this specific category, up from 46 at the start of the year. The data suggests that while federal legislation may be slower to move, state governments are prioritizing immediate regulatory action to mitigate the impact of deepfakes on public trust and safety.

Election Integrity and Political Campaigns

A primary driver of the 2026 legislative wave is the potential for deepfakes to manipulate election outcomes. Public Citizen notes that synthetic media could be released days or hours before an election, leaving no time for debunking and potentially misleading voters. In response, state legislatures are passing urgently needed legislation to regulate deepfakes in elections, often with bipartisan support.

The expansion of regulations targeting political campaigns is evident in the increase from 28 to 33 states regulating this area within a single year. These laws typically aim to ensure transparency and prevent the use of deceptive synthetic media to alter election results. The focus on election integrity distinguishes these state-level efforts from broader federal AI governance frameworks, which may take a more holistic approach to AI regulation.

Nonconsensual Imagery and Child Protection

Beyond election concerns, state legislatures are heavily focused on the proliferation of nonconsensual sexual content and child sexual abuse material (CSAM). Ballotpedia’s annual report highlights that lawmakers are addressing the distribution of deceptive political communications and election disinformation, as well as property rights infringement and harassment. The criminalization of the possession, creation, and distribution of such material is a common theme in recent state bills.

Specific attention is being paid to computer-generated material that resembles real persons, even when no actual individual was directly harmed. For example, Wyoming’s HB 102 aims to protect children from deepfakes and exploitative images, though verification of its full provisions requires further review. This focus on nonconsensual imagery reflects a broader societal concern about the misuse of deepfake technology for harassment and exploitation.

FIELD NOTE

Federal vs. State Regulatory Divergence

The absence of a single national deepfake law has led to a patchwork of state statutes layered on top of a growing but incomplete federal floor. Federal efforts, such as S.1837, have faced procedural hurdles; an earlier version died in the House before the end of the 118th Congress, and the reintroduced bill passed the Senate unanimously in January 2026 but remains pending in the House. This delay allows states to move independently, often resulting in a fragmented regulatory environment.

State laws vary significantly in their penalties and enforcement mechanisms. For instance, Michigan’s MCL 169.247 carries disclosure duties and misdemeanor penalties of up to $1,000 or 93 days. In contrast, other states may impose felony charges or civil damages. This divergence creates challenges for compliance and enforcement, requiring stakeholders to navigate a complex web of local regulations. The federal TAKE IT DOWN Act is also part of this evolving landscape, but state-level actions remain the primary driver of immediate regulatory change.

  • No single national deepfake law exists; state statutes are layered on a federal floor.
  • S.1837 passed the Senate in January 2026 but is pending in the House.
  • State penalties vary, ranging from misdemeanors to felonies and civil damages.
