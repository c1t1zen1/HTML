California’s 2026 AI Companion Rules Put Therapy Chatbots Under Self-Harm Safeguards

California's SB 243, effective Jan 1 2026, forces companion chatbots to disclose AI, add self-harm crisis protocols, and file annual suicide-risk reports—reshaping mental-health AI design and liability.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

California's SB 243—the first-in-the-nation companion chatbot law—took effect January 1, 2026, after Governor Gavin Newsom signed it on October 13, 2025. The law targets AI systems that form adaptive, human-like relationships with users, forcing operators to disclose that the chatbot is not human, implement self-harm and suicide prevention protocols, and file annual reports on chatbot-related suicidal ideation. FIELD NOTE

What SB 243 Requires of Companion Chatbots

California SB 243, signed October 13, 2025, and effective January 1, 2026, is the first state law to mandate safety safeguards for companion chatbots—AI systems designed to form adaptive, human-like relationships with users. The law applies only to companion chatbots and explicitly excludes many customer service and business operations tools, video game chatbots, and voice-activated assistants. Operators must disclose that the chatbot is not human when a user could reasonably be misled, maintain and publish a protocol addressing suicidal ideation and self-harm that refers users to crisis services, and, for minors, include recurring break reminders and content restrictions. Annual reporting on the connection between chatbot use and suicidal ideation is required to the state Office of Suicide Prevention.

Self-Harm Safeguards and Crisis Escalation

The core of SB 243's mental-health protections is a mandated protocol for addressing suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm. When a user's messages contain indicators of suicidal ideation, self-harm, or acute mental health crisis, the platform must display the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and other crisis resources. Operators must implement a notification that refers users to crisis service providers, and the law requires annual reporting on the connection between chatbot use and suicidal ideation to help get a clearer picture of risk. These requirements turn crisis escalation from an optional feature into a legal duty, forcing chatbot designers to build detection and referral pipelines that can respond in real time.

  • Protocol must address suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm (sources 1,2,3,7,11,12)
  • Must display 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and other crisis resources when indicators are present (source 6)
  • Annual reporting to the state Office of Suicide Prevention on chatbot-use and suicidal ideation (sources 2,3,5)

Redesigning Mental-Health Chatbots for Compliance

Compliance with SB 243 will reshape how mental-health chatbots are designed. The AI-disclosure requirement means users must be told they are interacting with a machine, not a human, whenever they could reasonably be misled—a direct challenge to chatbots that deliberately mimic empathy. For minors, the law adds recurring break reminders and content restrictions, acknowledging that prolonged, emotionally intense conversations can be harmful. These safeguards push designers toward transparent, bounded interactions rather than open-ended companionship, and they create new data-collection obligations around crisis indicators that must be reported annually.

  • AI disclosure required when users could reasonably be misled (sources 1,3,7,10)
  • Minor protections include recurring break reminders and content restrictions (sources 3,7,9)
  • Annual reporting creates data-collection and monitoring obligations (sources 2,3,5)

Platform Liability and the New Compliance Burden

SB 243 imposes operational and reporting requirements that go beyond simple disclosure. Operators must maintain and publish crisis protocols, which means they can be held accountable for failing to act when a user expresses self-harm intent. The annual reporting requirement to the state Office of Suicide Prevention creates a formal oversight channel, and non-compliance could expose platforms to regulatory action. New York has passed parallel companion-chatbot laws, signaling a broader trend that multiplies compliance complexity for companies operating across states. The precise liability contours—whether a private right of action exists—are not fully settled, but the statutory duties are now concrete.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence is clear: California SB 243 took effect on January 1, 2026, making it the first state law to mandate companion-chatbot safeguards. The statutory duties—AI disclosure, self-harm crisis protocols, minor protections, and annual reporting—are now binding for covered platforms. What remains uncertain is how these rules will reshape mental-health chatbot design in practice. The law forces crisis escalation to be a legal duty, but the effectiveness of detection algorithms and referral pipelines will depend on implementation. Platform liability is also evolving: while the statute creates clear operational duties, the full scope of legal exposure—including any private right of action—is not yet settled. As New York and other states follow suit, companies face a patchwork of obligations. The annual reports filed with California's Office of Suicide Prevention will provide the first empirical window into how chatbot use connects to suicidal ideation, but that data will take time to accumulate. Until then, designers and operators should treat SB 243 as a baseline for transparent, crisis-aware AI companionship.
