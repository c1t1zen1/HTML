FIELD NOTE

The law’s first test is scope

That scope matters for therapy-oriented products. A chatbot marketed or designed as an ongoing emotional companion may face a different compliance analysis from a narrowly bounded customer-service tool. One legal summary says the statute excludes many customer-service and business-operations tools, video-game chatbots, and voice-activated assistants, but the supplied evidence does not resolve how every therapy product would be classified. ↗ Where it could go

  • Published crisis protocols could give users and regulators a clearer account of how covered chatbots handle self-harm disclosures. [5][9]
  • Required crisis-service referrals could provide a more consistent path from a chatbot conversation to human support. [5][8]
  • Non-human disclosures could reduce the risk that users mistake a companion chatbot for a person or clinician. [5][8]
  • Annual reporting could create a recurring mechanism for reviewing operators’ stated suicide-prevention safeguards. [9]

△ What could go wrong

  • A written self-harm protocol may not guarantee reliable detection of indirect, ambiguous, or rapidly changing crisis language; the supplied sources do not provide performance standards.
  • Users may receive crisis referrals without the human handoff or emergency intervention they need, because those mechanisms are not established in the snippets. [5][9]
  • Products may fall into uncertain regulatory categories if their therapy functions, companion features, and marketing language overlap. [3][7][11]
  • Minor safeguards may vary in practical effectiveness because the supplied sources describe them at a high level and do not provide complete implementation details. [5][6][8]

◌ Watch next

  • Whether covered operators publish detailed suicidal-ideation and self-harm protocols, including the actual escalation steps and crisis-service references. [5][8]
  • Whether product interfaces consistently disclose that the chatbot is not human when users could reasonably be misled. [5][8]
  • What California’s annual reports to the Office of Suicide Prevention actually measure, and whether they disclose operational outcomes rather than only policy statements. [9]
  • Whether the California proposal targeting companies that market chatbots as therapy advances, changes, or becomes law. [11]
Closing read

Conclusion

The more consequential question for therapy-oriented systems is implementation. The evidence does not show that the law guarantees accurate self-harm detection, clinician involvement, emergency intervention, or therapeutic safety. Forecast: scrutiny is likely to concentrate on whether published protocols correspond to actual model behavior during crisis conversations, while the separate proposal concerning AI therapist marketing could expand oversight if enacted. [11]

Five takeaways

  • SB 243 is described as signed in 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, making 2026 the first active compliance period identified by the supplied sources. [1][5][8]
  • The law targets companion chatbots, not necessarily every chatbot; summaries describe exclusions for many customer-service, business, video-game, and voice-assistant systems. [3][7]
  • Self-harm and suicide safeguards include a described duty to maintain and publish a protocol that refers users to crisis services. [5][6]
  • The supplied evidence does not prove that California requires a specific detection algorithm, clinician handoff, emergency-services dispatch, or response-time guarantee.
  • A separate California bill concerning AI therapist marketing could create additional scrutiny, but the supplied snippet does not establish its final status or text. [11]
