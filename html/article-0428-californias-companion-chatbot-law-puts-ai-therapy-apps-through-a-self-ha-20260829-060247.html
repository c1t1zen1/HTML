California’s Companion-Chatbot Law Puts AI Therapy Apps Through a Self-Harm Safeguard Test

California’s SB 243 turns chatbot self-harm safety from a disclosure issue into an operational test: maintain intervention protocols, provide crisis referrals, and publish what those protocols do.

August 29, 2026 · 6:02 AM PDT · 6 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Mental Health & Therapy
AI therapymental healthchatbot regulation
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

California Senate Bill 243 became effective January 1, 2026, establishing requirements for companion chatbots around transparency, safety, and behavioral integrity. The supplied evidence identifies a specific self-harm safeguard: an operator must maintain a protocol intended to prevent the production of suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content, including crisis-service referrals when a user expresses such concerns.[1][4][7][11]

This article treats the California Legislative Information excerpts as the primary evidence and uses legal, professional, policy, and industry summaries for context. The material supports analysis of the law’s stated protocol and disclosure requirements, but it does not establish whether any particular AI therapy app legally qualifies as a companion chatbot, how regulators will evaluate protocol quality, or whether existing products comply.

Assignment angle: The angle focuses on California’s newly active companion-chatbot safeguards and whether AI therapy products can operationalize self-harm intervention requirements beyond general disclosure rules.

FIELD NOTE

The law’s central test is operational, not merely informational

SB 243 states that an operator must prevent a companion chatbot from engaging with users unless the operator maintains a protocol for preventing the production of suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content. The provision specifically includes notifying a user and referring that user to crisis service providers, including a suicide hotline or crisis text line, when the user expresses suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm.[1][3]

That language goes beyond a general notice that an AI system is not human or that it is not a therapist. The supplied summaries describe transparency and disclosure duties, but the statutory excerpt adds a behavior-linked condition: the platform must have a response protocol tied to expressions of self-harm or suicide.[5][11]

  • Operators must maintain a protocol addressing suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content.[1][3]
  • The stated protocol includes crisis-service notification and referral when a user expresses qualifying concerns.[1][3]
  • General transparency disclosures do not, by themselves, describe the full safeguard identified in the statutory excerpt.[5][11]

FIELD NOTE

Why AI therapy products face a higher practical bar

The supplied evidence does not say that every AI therapy product is covered by SB 243. It does, however, create a relevant implementation question for products designed to provide adaptive, human-like responses and sustain social relationships, which one industry summary identifies as characteristics of companion chatbots.[12]

For an AI therapy product, the meaningful test would be whether its safety design can detect or respond to user expressions covered by the law without continuing an unsafe interaction. The sources do not provide a technical standard for detection accuracy, escalation timing, human review, localization, recordkeeping, or false-positive handling. Those details therefore remain open implementation questions rather than established legal requirements in the supplied material.

  • The evidence does not resolve whether named AI therapy apps fall within SB 243’s companion-chatbot category.
  • Products with adaptive, human-like responses and relationship-oriented interaction may face direct relevance from the law’s companion-chatbot framework.[12]
  • The supplied sources do not specify measurable performance standards for self-harm detection or intervention.

FIELD NOTE

Publication creates a testable accountability trail

The bill summary states that operators must publish details about their self-harm-prevention protocol on their internet website.[10] That requirement gives users, clinicians, researchers, and policymakers a public point of comparison instead of limiting safety claims to general product messaging.

The evidence does not show what current operators have published or whether disclosures will be sufficiently detailed to evaluate real-world behavior. A public protocol can still be vague, incomplete, or disconnected from the system’s actual responses. The likely accountability value therefore depends on whether published material describes concrete intervention behavior rather than only repeating that safety measures exist.

  • Operators must publish details about the self-harm-prevention protocol, according to the bill summary.[10]
  • Public protocol descriptions could make safety commitments easier to compare.
  • The supplied material does not establish a required format, audit process, or enforcement metric for those publications.

FIELD NOTE

Youth protection is part of the regulatory context, not proof of product performance

Several supplied summaries frame SB 243 as a response to concerns about AI companions, especially those used by minors. The Future of Privacy Forum describes disclosures, safety protocols, and additional safeguards when the user is known to be a minor, while the legislative announcement reports bipartisan passage and the January 1, 2026 effective date.[5][7]

That context matters for therapy products that may be used by young people, but it does not prove that any product has successfully implemented the safeguards. The sources also do not provide outcome data, compliance findings, or evidence that crisis referrals consistently reach users in need.

  • The supplied policy summaries identify minors as a particular focus of additional safeguards.[2][5]
  • SB 243’s reported effective date is January 1, 2026.[4][7][11]
  • No supplied source reports measured compliance or clinical outcomes for AI therapy products.

Closing read

Conclusion

SB 243 establishes a fact-based baseline: covered companion-chatbot operators must maintain a protocol addressing suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content, provide crisis-service referrals when users express those concerns, and publish protocol details.[1][3][10] That is materially different from relying only on a disclosure that an AI is not human or not a therapist.[5][11]

The evidence does not yet show how California will classify AI therapy apps, judge protocol effectiveness, or enforce implementation. The projection is that public protocol requirements could push products toward more explicit and reviewable self-harm interventions, but that benefit depends on whether published commitments correspond to actual system behavior. Readers should treat product compliance and safety performance as unresolved until those signals are documented.

Five takeaways

  • SB 243 requires covered operators to maintain a protocol addressing suicidal ideation, suicide, or self-harm content.[1][3]
  • The stated intervention includes notifying users and referring them to crisis-service providers when they express qualifying concerns.[1][3]
  • The law’s self-harm provision is operational in character and cannot be reduced to a general disclosure requirement based on the supplied text.[1][5][11]
  • Operators must publish details about the self-harm-prevention protocol, creating a public accountability point.[10]
  • The supplied evidence establishes the legal baseline and effective date but does not establish coverage, compliance, technical performance, or clinical outcomes for any specific AI therapy app.[4][7][10][12]
