Smart-Home Sensors Face EU Data-Grouping Test as Consent Rules Tighten

EU Data Act's September 2026 'access by design' rules force smart-home sensor data to be grouped by purpose for consent, reshaping AI automation.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article examines how the EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854), applicable to newly placed connected products from September 12, 2026, introduces 'access by design' duties that require smart-home devices to provide users with easy, secure, free access to their data. The distinct angle is the emerging legal requirement that sensor data be grouped by purpose for user consent, which creates a novel compliance test for AI-driven home automation systems. Evidence comes from regulatory summaries, academic analyses, and compliance guides, including sources on the Data Act's design duties, GDPR interaction, and smart-home-specific challenges such as aggregating sensor data and obtaining valid consent in shared environments.

FIELD NOTE

The Data Act's 'Access by Design' Duty and Its Purpose-Grouping Implication

From September 12, 2026, all new connected products placed on the EU market must be designed so that users can directly, securely, and freely obtain the data they generate (Sources 2, 3, 6, 9). The EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) explicitly requires 'access by design' principles, meaning manufacturers must map data flows and integrate APIs to allow user access (Sources 2, 7). While the Data Act is fully compliant with GDPR, it goes beyond GDPR's portability right: GDPR covers personal data processed on the basis of consent or contract, whereas the Data Act covers all data generated by connected devices regardless of the processing basis (Source 10).

  • Access by design applies to 'newly placed' connected products from 12 September 2026, including smart-home devices, wearables, and IoT products (Sources 1, 6).
  • Users must be able to access, download, and share device data easily, securely, and free of charge (Sources 2, 6).
  • Manufacturers must map data flows and integrate APIs to comply by 2026 (Source 7).
  • The Data Act covers all data generated by connected devices, not just personal data, unlike GDPR portability (Source 10).

The Purpose-Grouping Test for AI-Driven Smart-Home Sensors

The emerging legal requirement is that smart-home sensor data must be grouped by purpose for user consent. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has clarified that the GDPR's data-minimization and purpose-limitation rules are technology-neutral and apply to a wide array of devices (Source 4). An IoT company cannot simply extract sensor data from a smart refrigerator or thermostat without informing the data subject and, where required, obtaining consent—unless such access is strictly necessary for the service requested (Source 4). This creates a distinct compliance test for AI-driven home automation, where sensors often collect data for multiple purposes (e.g., energy management, security, predictive maintenance) and AI may aggregate or re-purpose data.

  • The EDPB has clarified that data extraction from smart devices requires informing users and obtaining consent unless strictly necessary for the service (Source 4).
  • AI-driven automation systems must segment sensor data by purpose (e.g., energy, security, comfort) to offer granular consent choices.
  • Purpose-grouping is not yet an explicit statutory term in the Data Act, but GDPR's purpose limitation and EDPB guidance imply it as a practical compliance step.
  • Manufacturers face a higher burden to map data flows and design APIs that support purpose-specific user consent (Source 7).

Challenges in Shared Environments and Aggregated Sensor Data

Applying GDPR to smart-home infrastructure is not straightforward, especially in shared environments like apartment buildings or multi-occupant homes (Source 8). Challenges include obtaining valid consent from building occupants for data collection, determining the data controller (building owner vs. service provider), and implementing individuals' rights like access or erasure in systems that aggregate sensor data (Source 8). The Data Act's 'access by design' does not resolve these issues; it adds a layer of technical and legal complexity, because AI-driven systems often process aggregated data that may not map neatly to individual consent choices.

  • Shared environments complicate consent: valid consent from each occupant is difficult to obtain (Source 8).
  • Determining the data controller (building owner vs. service provider) remains unresolved (Source 8).
  • Aggregated sensor data makes implementing individual rights like access or erasure difficult (Source 8).
  • The Data Act's access-by-design duties do not override GDPR's purpose-limitation and consent requirements (Source 10).

Non-Consent Models and Legitimate Interests: Alternatives to Purpose-Grouped Consent

Expert perspectives reveal that some smart-home deployments, especially for vulnerable persons, rely on non-consent models. For instance, a local authority may not ask for explicit consent for a particular sensor but instead rely on its duty of care and the 'best interests of the individual' (Source 11). A UK solicitor argued that legitimate interests are popular because consent can be transitory or affected by conditions like dementia, where a person may consent at one moment and withdraw it later (Source 11). These alternatives could reduce reliance on purpose-grouped consent, but the Data Act's access-by-design duty still applies to all generated data, regardless of the legal basis for processing (Source 10).

  • Non-consent models exist where duty of care and 'best interests' are used instead of explicit consent (Source 11).
  • Legitimate interests are considered a practical alternative, especially for vulnerable persons where consent may be unstable (Source 11).
  • The Data Act does not require a specific legal basis for data access; it applies to all generated data (Source 10).
  • Purpose-grouping remains relevant under GDPR even if the Data Act itself does not mandate it explicitly.

FIELD NOTE

Cybersecurity and Regulatory Interplay: The Cyber Resilience Act

The Cyber Resilience Act makes cybersecurity a condition of EU market access for connected products, with vulnerability reporting obligations from 11 September 2026 and full requirements from 11 December 2027 (Source 5). This creates a parallel compliance timeline with the Data Act. Manufacturers of smart-home sensors must not only provide user access but also ensure that their products are cyber-resilient. The interaction between these regulations means that AI-driven automation systems must be designed with both security and data-access features from the outset, not as afterthoughts.

  • The Cyber Resilience Act requires cybersecurity as a market-access condition (Source 5).
  • Vulnerability reporting obligations begin 11 September 2026; full requirements from 11 December 2027 (Source 5).
  • Manufacturers must integrate cybersecurity and data-access by design to meet both regulations.
  • The Data Act and Cyber Resilience Act together impose a dual compliance burden for smart-home products.

Closing read

Conclusion

The EU Data Act's 'access by design' duty, effective September 12, 2026, is a clear regulatory mandate for smart-home products. However, the purpose-grouping requirement for sensor data is not explicitly stated in the Data Act itself; it emerges from GDPR's purpose limitation and the EDPB's technology-neutral interpretation. This creates a distinct compliance test: AI-driven home automation must separate data streams by purpose (e.g., energy, security, comfort) to allow users to consent to each purpose separately. The evidence shows that this is not yet a settled statutory rule but a practical necessity inferred from GDPR and EDPB guidance.

Five takeaways

  • The EU Data Act's access-by-design duty applies to all new connected products placed on the market from September 12, 2026 (Sources 1, 2, 3, 6, 9).
  • The Data Act covers all data generated by connected devices, regardless of processing basis, unlike GDPR's portability right (Source 10).
  • The EDPB has clarified that extracting sensor data without informing users or obtaining consent is not allowed unless strictly necessary for the service (Source 4).
  • Purpose-grouping for consent is not explicitly stated in the Data Act but is implied by GDPR's purpose limitation and EDPB guidance (Sources 4, 10).
  • Shared environments and aggregated sensor data complicate consent and data access, and the Cyber Resilience Act adds a parallel compliance deadline (Sources 5, 8).
