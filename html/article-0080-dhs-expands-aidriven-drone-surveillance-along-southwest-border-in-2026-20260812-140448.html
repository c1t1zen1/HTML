FIELD NOTE

AI‑Enhanced Tower and Mobile Surveillance Deployment

GDIT received a $71 million delivery order for Relocatable Autonomous Surveillance Towers (RAST) to be installed at multiple sites along the southwest border under the $1.8 billion Consolidated Tower & Surveillance Equipment (CTSE) contract. In April 2026, GDIT announced that these towers earned U.S. Customs and Border Protection certification, meeting autonomy requirements set by OBBBA for funding and deployment, with PureActiv providing the autonomy software. Separately, DHS is pursuing a $1 billion blanket purchase agreement with Palantir and allocating over $100 million for AI‑powered mobile surveillance trucks, while planning for 198 AI‑enabled border towers. The agency also published a pre‑solicitation notice for the Modular Mobile Surveillance System (M2S2), a vehicle‑based platform that can transform into an autonomous observation tower using AI, radar, cameras, and wireless networking.

Operational Testing and AI Capabilities in the Field

Analysts at an Army base in the Arizona desert have employed an MQ‑9 Predator B drone equipped with AI‑powered radar to monitor migrant crossings, demonstrating wartime‑derived technology being repurposed for border enforcement. Meanwhile, CBP’s small drones program requires Border Patrol agents to complete online training, pass an FAA‑administered test for a drone pilot’s license, and undergo additional CBP‑specific instruction on drone operations and regulations. The CBP AI use case inventory highlights an obstacle‑avoidance feature that processes live video to help ground pilots avoid collisions with structures, vehicles, trees, and wires.

Policy Framework, Funding, and Dual‑Use Infrastructure

In January 2026, DHS launched a new office to advance drone and counter‑drone technologies, citing the need to secure the border, protect infrastructure, and keep Americans safe during events such as the nation’s 250th birthday and the FIFA 2026 World Cup. This initiative builds on a 2018 law signed by President Trump that granted DHS components authority to detect and mitigate drones. Complementarily, DHS unveiled a $1.5 billion drone surveillance office that will provide ICE access to drone footage; drones originally procured for World Cup security are slated for reuse in immigration enforcement, and the supporting infrastructure is designed as dual‑use with currently no meaningful federal restrictions on how domestic drone surveillance data may be used.

Privacy Implications and Advocacy Warnings

Privacy advocates warn that DHS’s record AI‑driven surveillance budget—covering airport facial recognition, predictive analytics that mine commercial data, and other tools—could reshape border controls and expose business travelers to unprecedented data harvesting and algorithmic risk scoring. Reports indicate that DHS has filed zero privacy impact assessments for its surveillance purchases this year, raising concerns about oversight and the potential for mission creep as surveillance technologies expand beyond their original security purpose.

Closing read

Conclusion

The factual record shows that DHS is actively scaling AI‑enhanced drone surveillance along the Southwest Border through funded tower contracts, certified autonomous systems, field‑tested AI radar on MQ‑9 Predator B drones, and expanding small‑drone operator training. Policy moves include a new drone‑focused office, a substantial drone surveillance budget, and a dual‑use infrastructure strategy that leverages event‑related drones for immigration enforcement. These actions are documented in government announcements, contractor disclosures, and official program descriptions.

Looking ahead, the projections center on privacy and civil‑liberties impacts. Advocates warn that the absence of privacy impact assessments, coupled with extensive data harvesting and algorithmic scoring, could erode travelers’ rights and enable mission creep. The dual‑use design and lack of federal restrictions on domestic drone data suggest potential expansion beyond border security, raising concerns about oversight and the permanence of surveillance records. While the technology promises improved detection and safety benefits, its deployment will need to be weighed against the documented risks of privacy infringement and insufficient accountability.
