The supplied evidence points to a labor-contract test for AI virtual presenters: whether a performer’s voice, appearance, or digital replica can be licensed, reused, substituted, or transferred under enforceable terms. FIELD NOTE

Consent is only one part of the bargain

The supplied union material frames AI replica use around more than permission at the point of capture. The Interactive Media Agreement is described as requiring consent and disclosure, while also allowing performers to suspend consent for generating new material during a strike.[2][3] That language makes control over future generation and labor-action conditions part of the contractual picture.

SAG-AFTRA’s Narrativ announcement says the agreement includes informed consent, compensation, and individual control requirements for digital voice replicas used in digital audio advertising.[5] A separate SAG-AFTRA announcement describes its agreement with Replica Studios as enabling members to create and license voice replicas under a fair and ethical agreement.[12] Another supplied record describes voluntary participation, informed consent, and minimum scale compensation for each use in the Replica Studios partnership, though that description comes from a secondary data record rather than the union page itself.[4]

These snippets do not establish a universal compensation formula for every virtual presenter. They do establish a direction: a license may need to specify who authorizes use, what uses are paid, and whether the performer can control later generation or suspend permission in defined circumstances.

  • The sources describe voluntary replica licensing models for interactive media and voice use.[4][12]
  • The supplied snippets do not establish one compensation schedule applicable to all presenter deployments.

Synthetic substitution expands the dispute beyond a performer’s own replica

Trade coverage says SAG-AFTRA sought control over synthetic performers: digital avatars that do not resemble a real-life actor.[8] Another report says the new contract includes a penalty for using a synthetic performer instead of a real actor, alongside rules concerning digital replicas, replica security, and notice when an actor’s data is licensed to a third party for AI training.[10]

A Variety snippet says the contract allows producers to use a digital avatar and that the union has argued its language, together with an arbitration provision, would limit AI-replica use to a handful of edge cases.[7] The supplied material does not state the complete definitions, thresholds, penalty amount, or arbitration procedure. It therefore supports identifying these as negotiation and compliance pressure points, not describing their precise legal effect.

For AI virtual presenters, the distinction between an authorized replica and a synthetic replacement could become commercially important. That is a forecast from the supplied contract themes, not a confirmed rule specifically governing every presenter product.

Licensing models offer a possible path, but control remains the test

The supplied union announcements show that negotiated licensing models already exist for AI-generated voice replicas. The Narrativ arrangement addresses digital audio advertising, while the Replica Studios announcement addresses creation and licensing of voice replicas for interactive uses.[5][12] A secondary source likewise describes voluntary participation and compensation for replica use.[4]

Those examples may provide a template for virtual-presenter businesses that want performer-backed avatars: define the permitted uses, identify compensation, disclose the replica, and preserve individual control. That is a possible operational model, not evidence that the same terms automatically apply to video presenters, commercials, television, film, games, or other categories.

The forecasted market consequence is a shift from treating an avatar as a production asset to treating it as a contracted performance right. Whether that shift reduces disputes will depend on the final agreement status, contract classification, and enforcement mechanisms, all of which remain incompletely specified by the supplied snippets.

Closing read

Conclusion

The strongest fact pattern is that SAG-AFTRA’s AI policy work increasingly addresses consent, disclosure, compensation, individual control, strike-related suspension, replica security, synthetic substitution, and third-party data licensing.[2][3][5][10] The union and partner announcements also show that voluntary, compensated replica licensing is being used as an agreement model in voice and interactive-media contexts.[4][12]

The unresolved issue is the final scope and status of the 2026 studio rules. The supplied sources conflict between tentative-deal and ratified-contract descriptions.[7][9][11] It is therefore a forecast, not an established fact, that virtual-presenter companies will face a single consolidated rulebook. The evidence instead supports a more cautious conclusion: deployments involving union performers should be designed around explicit authorization, compensation, use limits, disclosure, and control until governing contracts and classifications are confirmed.
