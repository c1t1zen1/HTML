FIELD NOTE

Judicial Pushback Against State Authority

Federal courts are increasingly skeptical of state laws that regulate political deepfakes, citing First Amendment concerns. In a significant ruling on January 30, 2026, a federal court permanently blocked Hawaii's election deepfake law. The court determined that the law's mandatory disclaimer requirement, which applied even to satire, imposed unconstitutional restrictions on political speech. This decision aligns with earlier actions in California, where a federal judge struck down a law aimed at restricting AI-generated deepfake content during elections on August 5. These rulings suggest that broad prohibitions or disclosure mandates may struggle to survive strict scrutiny when they encroach on protected political expression.

Not all state laws have faced identical fates, however. In Louisiana, the governor vetoed an election deepfake bill in July 2024 on First Amendment grounds, preventing it from becoming law. Conversely, other jurisdictions have seen core prohibitions upheld. For instance, a law prohibiting the creation and publication of deepfake videos within 30 days of an election with intent to injure a candidate was upheld in certain contexts. This mixed judicial response creates a patchwork of enforceable standards, complicating compliance for platforms and creators operating across state lines.

The Federal Preemption Battle

Beyond judicial challenges, state deepfake laws face existential threats from federal legislative efforts aimed at preemption. Supporters of federal intervention argue that a unified national standard is necessary to avoid a confusing patchwork of conflicting state regulations. In May 2025, the House of Representatives passed the "One Big Beautiful" bill, which proposed a ten-year moratorium on state-level AI laws. This move reflects a broader strategy by the Trump administration and some Republicans in Congress to force through AI preemption laws that would render state laws null without establishing meaningful federal protections.

Critics of these preemption efforts contend that they leave elections vulnerable in the absence of national safeguards. With 30 states now having laws to regulate election deepfakes, the prospect of a federal moratorium could nullify significant state-level progress. The debate centers on whether federal preemption serves to create clarity or to dismantle existing state authority without providing adequate alternatives. As of early 2026, the tension between state legislative activity and federal preemption attempts remains unresolved, with significant implications for election integrity frameworks.

Fragmented State Standards and Disclosure Gaps

State-level approaches to regulating election deepfakes vary significantly, particularly regarding disclosure requirements and temporal restrictions. As of January 9, 2026, three states with laws prohibiting deepfake distribution before an election did not include exceptions for materials containing disclosure statements. This lack of a safe harbor for disclosed synthetic media contrasts with other jurisdictions that may allow such content if properly labeled. California and Texas, for example, have passed laws directly prohibiting deceptive deepfake content in political campaigns, focusing on synthetic media that misrepresents a candidate’s speech or actions.

Other states are expanding their regulatory reach. Maryland’s new law, effective June 1, 2026, authorizes the state administrator of elections to act upon receiving credible reports of election-related deepfakes. Meanwhile, some proposals extend prohibitions to 120 days before an election and add platform takedown duties for deceptive AI-generated content. Minnesota and Texas prohibit distribution within a certain number of days of an election, but the specific windows and exceptions differ. This fragmentation forces platforms to navigate a complex web of state-specific rules, increasing compliance costs and legal uncertainty.
