C2PA 2.3 extends provenance to live video

The C2PA specification reached version 2.3 in late 2025 or early 2026, adding live video provenance support for broadcast and streaming. One source says the update was released in December 2025 and extended provenance to live streaming via CMAF segment signing. Another source says the specification reached version 2.3 in February 2026, and C2PA's own announcement on February 9, 2026 says Content Credentials 2.3 enabled live video. The exact release timing is inconsistent across sources, but the capability itself is clearly documented.

C2PA also celebrated its fifth anniversary with over 6,000 members and affiliates now using Content Credentials. The 2.3 specification update adds live video provenance support for broadcast and streaming, plus improvements to creator provenance data. The standard is also on an ISO fast-track, which could broaden adoption beyond the current coalition.

Verification reality: metadata stripping and transcoding

Signing content is only the first step in a provenance workflow. The chain breaks the moment platforms strip metadata during upload and transcoding. This is a central risk for any live-video provenance system, including those aimed at virtual presenters. Even if a stream is signed at the source, downstream platforms may remove the credentials before the viewer sees the content.

C2PA 2.3 closes the gap by enabling provenance to be asserted and validated as content flows through the video pipeline, supporting trust without interrupting delivery. But the practical verification reality depends on whether platforms preserve the metadata. The evidence does not indicate that major social or streaming platforms have committed to preserving C2PA credentials in live workflows.

FIELD NOTE

Unified Streaming Trusted Media as an early implementation

Unified Streaming has introduced Trusted Media, a content provenance system capable of attaching C2PA credentials to live, on-demand and dynamically assembled video streams immediately before distribution. The solution enables broadcasters, streaming platforms, and premium content owners to attach verifiable authenticity information dynamically to live, on-demand, and virtual channel streams just in time as they are prepared for distribution.

The solution will be demonstrated at IBC 2026, September 11-14. The launch comes as European media organizations adapt to the EU AI Act's transparency obligations, which create regulatory pressure for provenance and disclosure of AI-generated content. Trusted Media is positioned as a just-in-time signing layer rather than a deepfake detector, so it does not by itself verify whether a presenter is human or synthetic.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence establishes that C2PA 2.3 added live video provenance and that Unified Streaming launched Trusted Media as a commercial implementation. It also establishes a critical failure mode: metadata stripping during upload and transcoding can break the provenance chain. These are facts from the supplied sources.

What remains projection is whether avatar-generated livestreams can be verified in practice. The sources do not document any avatar-specific workflow, and the term 'virtual channel streams' in Trusted Media is not equivalent to virtual presenters. The EU AI Act creates regulatory pressure, but enforcement and technical standards are still evolving. The release date conflict between December 2025 and February 2026 also suggests that the timeline for live provenance adoption is not fully settled.

For virtual presenters, the key test will be whether provenance metadata is preserved end-to-end and whether the signing step explicitly declares synthetic content. Until platforms preserve C2PA credentials and avatar workflows adopt the standard, authenticity claims for avatar livestreams remain unverified.
