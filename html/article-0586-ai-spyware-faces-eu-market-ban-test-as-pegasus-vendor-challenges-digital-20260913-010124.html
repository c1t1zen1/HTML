AI Spyware Faces EU Market Ban Test as Pegasus Vendor Challenges Digital Markets Act

NSO Group faces EU market access challenges under the Digital Markets Act, while Meta seeks contempt sanctions for violating US court injunctions.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Recent legal and regulatory developments highlight a growing tension between European digital sovereignty laws and the commercial operations of AI-enhanced spyware vendors. Evidence from the German Marshall Fund and European Digital Rights indicates that the EU is scrutinizing how vendors like NSO Group maintain access to the internal market, with some member states hosting subsidiaries that may benefit from export licenses potentially violating EU law. This regulatory pressure coincides with aggressive litigation in the United States, where Meta has filed contempt motions against NSO Group for allegedly violating permanent injunctions against targeting WhatsApp users.

The investigation draws on legal trackers, regulatory overviews, and news reports to map the dual-front battle facing spyware developers. While the Digital Markets Act aims to ensure fair competition, its intersection with human rights sanctions and export controls remains complex. Sources indicate that NSO Group’s chief stepped down in January 2026 amid US scrutiny, and Meta’s June 2026 contempt filing underscores the durability of legal barriers against these firms.

FIELD NOTE

EU Digital Sovereignty and Market Access Challenges

The European Union is increasingly leveraging its regulatory frameworks to restrict the operations of spyware vendors within its internal market. The German Marshall Fund notes that while some vendors have adapted by labeling products as "EU-regulated" to maintain market access, significant legal ambiguities remain. Specifically, member states such as Cyprus and Bulgaria are reported to host subsidiaries of major vendors like the Israeli NSO Group. These subsidiaries may benefit from export licenses that could be in violation of EU law, creating a regulatory loophole that the EU is now testing.

  • Cyprus and Bulgaria host subsidiaries of major spyware vendors.
  • Some vendors label products as "EU-regulated" to maintain market access.
  • Export licenses for these subsidiaries may violate EU law.

The Digital Markets Act as a Regulatory Lever

The Digital Markets Act (DMA), which entered into force in November 2022 and became largely applicable in May 2023, aims to ensure contestable and fair markets in the digital sector. While primarily designed to regulate gatekeepers, the DMA's evaluation process, required by May 3, 2026, provides a mechanism for broader scrutiny of digital supply chains. Recent developments in DMA implementation suggest a tightening of rules that could impact how surveillance technology vendors operate within the EU, particularly if their practices are deemed to undermine market fairness or security.

  • DMA entered into force on November 1, 2022.
  • European Commission must review DMA legislation by May 3, 2026.
  • DMA aims to ensure higher competition and contestability in digital markets.

US Legal Escalation: Meta’s Contempt Motion Against NSO

Parallel to EU regulatory pressures, legal actions in the United States have intensified. In June 2026, Meta filed a federal court contempt order against NSO Group, alleging that the spyware firm violated a permanent injunction barring it from targeting WhatsApp and its users. Meta claims that NSO Group ran new social engineering attempts against WhatsApp users within months of receiving the injunction. This move seeks to enforce the court's authority and potentially remove NSO from lists that might otherwise allow continued operational flexibility.

  • Meta filed a contempt motion against NSO Group on June 8, 2026.
  • NSO allegedly violated a permanent injunction against targeting WhatsApp.
  • Meta argues sanctions should remain in place due to continued violations.

Human Rights Sanctions and Corporate Accountability

Beyond market access and civil litigation, the EU’s human rights sanction regime is being considered as a tool to target malicious spyware vendors. Reports link Pegasus spyware to human rights abuses against political dissidents and activists. Legal analysts argue that even if NSO Group does not directly use the information obtained to carry out abuses, providing the technological tool that makes such abuses possible can be considered "providing support to" the abusing entities. This perspective strengthens the case for stringent sanctions and regulatory bans.

Conclusion

The convergence of EU digital sovereignty laws and US judicial enforcement creates a high-stakes environment for AI-enhanced spyware vendors. The evidence confirms that NSO Group faces simultaneous pressure from EU regulators scrutinizing market access and export licenses, and from US courts enforcing injunctions against WhatsApp targeting. The fact that some vendors are attempting to rebrand as "EU-regulated" suggests they are adapting to these pressures, but the underlying legal risks regarding human rights violations and injunction breaches remain acute. Future outcomes will depend on the EU's May 2026 DMA review and the US court's response to Meta's contempt motion.
