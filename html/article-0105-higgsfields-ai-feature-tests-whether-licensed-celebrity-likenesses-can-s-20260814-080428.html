Higgsfield’s AI Feature Tests Whether Licensed Celebrity Likenesses Can Scale Beyond Deepfakes

Higgsfield’s 110-minute AI film 'The Cully Hill Boys' uses licensed celebrity likenesses and an acquired script, testing whether authorized synthetic performers can scale beyond deepfakes.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The Cully Hill Boys, a 110-minute AI-generated action-comedy, premiered August 5 at The Glasshouse in New York. Higgsfield AI built it for roughly $2 million in four weeks, using likenesses of streamer N3on, UFC fighter Israel Adesanya, actor/UFC fighter Quinton 'Rampage' Jackson, and basketball influencer Matt Kiatipis—all under licensing agreements. The Verge reports Higgsfield acquired the script's AI production rights before production, and none of the actors shot scenes on set. This article synthesizes evidence from trade outlets, company announcements, and press coverage to examine whether this licensed-likeness model can move beyond unauthorized deepfakes.

Evidence was gathered from Forbes, Variety, The Verge, 36kr, LinkedIn, Bottle Rocket Content, Best.xiaohu.ai, The PR Net, and other outlets. These sources agree on the film's existence, budget, and open-source release, but they differ in emphasis: Variety and The Verge focus on licensing and production structure, while 36kr and LinkedIn add valuation and timeline projections. No source independently verifies the exact licensing terms or the $5B valuation, so those claims are treated as company or press projections.

Assignment angle: The 110-minute Cully Hill Boys shifts the debate from unauthorized synthetic performers to a commercially structured model combining licensed likenesses, an acquired screenplay, and AI-first production.

FIELD NOTE

The production model: licensed faces, acquired script, AI-first pipeline

Higgsfield's approach combines three distinct commercial moves: acquiring the AI production rights to a screenplay, signing likeness agreements with real celebrities, and running an AI-first production pipeline. According to The Verge, Higgsfield tapped streamer Mikyle 'N3on' Rafiq, basketball content creator Matt Kiatipis, and UFC fighter Israel Adesanya to 'star' as leads; each signed licensing agreements, though none shot scenes on set. Variety adds actor and UFC fighter Quinton 'Rampage' Jackson and basketball-focused influencer Matt Kiatipis to the cast. The film was produced for about $2 million in four weeks, per LinkedIn, and showcases Higgsfield's Cinema Studio toolkit and Seedance 2.5 feature, according to UA News. The result is a 110-minute feature that premiered at The Glasshouse in New York on August 5, 2026, as reported by Forbes and The PR Net.

Open-sourcing the pipeline: prompts, assets, and Claude skills

Higgsfield released The Cully Hill Boys for free and published the full production build under a study-only license. Bottle Rocket Content reports every prompt and asset behind the $2 million production is now public. Best.xiaohu.ai adds that Higgsfield Studio's write-up covers assets, prompts, performance, and lip-sync, and even makes three private Claude skills available to download. LinkedIn's analysis notes the study-only license lets anyone read the prompts. This open-sourcing is unusual for a commercial film and could accelerate research into AI filmmaking, though it also raises questions about how licensed likenesses interact with public prompt data.

What this means for the deepfake debate

The core distinction of The Cully Hill Boys is authorization. Instead of scraping or mimicking celebrities without consent, Higgsfield signed licensing agreements with real people for their likenesses. Variety's exclusive coverage frames this as a commercial model, not a loophole. The Verge emphasizes that none of the actors shot scenes on set, making the film a test of whether licensed synthetic performers can replace unauthorized deepfakes. The open-source release complicates the picture: while the film itself is authorized, the prompts and assets could be reused in ways that extend beyond the original licensing scope. The debate shifts from 'is it legal?' to 'can licensing scale?'

  • Licensing agreements replace unauthorized synthetic performers (Variety, The Verge).
  • Actors did not shoot scenes; likenesses were legally licensed (The Verge).

Closing read

Conclusion

The Cully Hill Boys is a concrete test of a licensed-likeness model for AI filmmaking. The evidence confirms that Higgsfield acquired a screenplay's AI rights, signed real celebrities for their likenesses, and produced a 110-minute feature for about $2 million in four weeks. It also confirms the film was released free and open-sourced, with prompts and assets made public. These are established facts from multiple outlets.

Projections are more fragile. The $5B valuation comes from 36kr and is not independently verified. Whether the model scales depends on celebrity willingness to license, legal clarity around AI likeness rights, and audience acceptance—none of which are settled. The open-source release adds a wildcard: it could accelerate innovation or enable misuse beyond the original licensing scope.

The shift from unauthorized deepfakes to licensed synthetic performers is real, but it is not a complete answer. The film's commercial performance, downstream use of its open-sourced assets, and regulatory responses will determine whether this becomes a durable production model or a one-off experiment.
